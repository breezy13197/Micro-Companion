Module 1 · Section 1

Viral fingerprint lab

Childhood rashes are easy to confuse. The most reliable first sort is not the rash but the virus itself: what is the genome, and is there an envelope? Those features decide what the virion must carry, and they point toward the clinical pattern.

Key points

  • Measles and mumps (paramyxoviruses) have negative-sense RNA, helical capsids, envelopes, and a polymerase packaged in the virion.
  • Rubella (a togavirus) has positive-sense RNA, an icosahedral capsid, and an envelope.
  • Parvovirus B19 has single-stranded DNA and no envelope. HHV-6 (a herpesvirus) has double-stranded DNA, an envelope, and two serotypes.

The two RNA-polymerase carriers are the paramyxoviruses. The only nonenveloped virus here is B19.

Module 1 · Section 2

Measles exposure timeline

Measles is among the most contagious viruses, and much of its spread happens before anyone knows what the illness is. Two ideas drive the response: the contagious window around the rash, and the short time windows for post-exposure options.

Key points

  • Contagious from about 4 days before to about 4 days after rash onset. Droplet and airborne spread, and virus can stay infectious in the air for up to 2 hours.
  • Airborne Precautions apply to a suspected case.
  • Post-exposure: MMR within 72 hours, or immune globulin within 6 days, when appropriate for the person. MMR is not given in pregnancy or severe immunocompromise.

Why a waiting room matters: the virus stays in the air after the patient has left.

Module 1 · Section 3

Rash, immunity, and complications

The measles rash is not the virus eating the skin. It is the immune system, in the form of cytotoxic T cells, attacking infected skin cells. That has a surprising consequence for who gets a rash, and for what measles does to immunity afterward.

Key points

  • Pathway: upper respiratory tract, blood, reticuloendothelial cells (a second round of replication), then skin. The viral fusion protein joins cells into giant cells.
  • Koplik spots can appear inside the cheeks before the rash. Immune amnesia means earlier immune memory can be erased.
  • Complications occur in about 30% of U.S. cases: diarrhea, otitis media, pneumonia, encephalitis, and, years later, SSPE. Vitamin A lowered deaths in children under 2.

Module 2 · Section 1

Mumps: where it goes

Mumps is best known for swollen parotid glands, but the virus does not stop there. Thinking in terms of tissues reached is more useful than memorizing a list: after the respiratory tract, which organs are in the path?

Key points

  • Spread is by respiratory droplets. After the respiratory tract, the virus reaches the parotid glands.
  • It can also involve the testes, ovaries, pancreas, and meninges. Orchitis occurs in postpubertal males, and sterility is rare and linked to bilateral orchitis.
  • Mumps meningitis is typically benign. There is no antiviral for mumps.

Module 2 · Section 2

Rubella: mother to fetus

Rubella is usually a mild illness for the person who has it. The reason it matters is the pregnant woman, because the virus that spreads in her blood can reach the placenta and the developing fetus.

Key points

  • Replication in the nasopharynx and local lymph nodes, then viremia, then skin and organs. The origin of the rash is uncertain.
  • First-trimester infection is linked to the most profound fetal harm (congenital rubella syndrome). Infants shed virus for months.
  • MMR is not given in pregnancy. Vaccinate before pregnancy or postpartum. Immune globulin is not routine.

Module 2 · Section 3

Who can receive MMR?

MMR contains live, weakened viruses. That is why it protects so well, and it is also why there are two groups who do not receive it: pregnant women and people who are severely immunocompromised.

Key points

  • Two doses: 12–15 months and 4–6 years. It protects against measles, mumps, and rubella.
  • It is not given in pregnancy or in severe immunocompromise.
  • A non-immune woman can be vaccinated before pregnancy or after delivery. After a measles exposure, MMR can be given within 72 hours when appropriate.

Module 3 · Section 1

B19 and the hydrops chain

Parvovirus B19 attacks one kind of cell, the red blood cell precursor. Almost everything clinically important about it follows from that: who becomes severely anemic, who needs IVIG, and what happens to a fetus.

Key points

  • B19 infects erythroblasts and halts red cell production. It spreads by the respiratory route and across the placenta. Slapped-cheek rash is the classic childhood sign.
  • Aplastic crisis occurs in sickle cell disease, thalassemia, or hereditary spherocytosis. Immunocompromised patients can develop chronic anemia.
  • Fetal risk is about 5–10%, highest at weeks 9–20. Fetal anemia can progress to heart failure and edema (hydrops), and most fetal infections resolve. Rash and arthritis are linked to immune complexes. Treatment is supportive, with transfusion or IVIG.

Module 3 · Section 2

Roseola and reactivation

Roseola is a benign childhood illness with a distinctive order of events. HHV-6 is also a good example of what latency means: the immune system can control a virus without removing it.

Key points

  • Roseola: high fever, then a rash that appears after the fever resolves. HHV-6 infects B and T cells and has two serotypes.
  • After infection the virus is latent. Lifelong immunity does not apply in the same way, because the virus can reactivate.
  • In transplant recipients, reactivation can cause encephalitis, pneumonia, and hepatitis. Foscarnet or ganciclovir are listed.

Module 4 · Section 1

The fecal-oral chain

Enteric viruses are shed in stool and swallowed by the next host. Everything they have in common, including how well they survive outside the body, makes sense once you picture that chain.

Key points

  • All enteric viruses spread by the fecal-oral route, have no envelope (so they survive stomach acid), and have RNA genomes.
  • Two groups: gastroenteritis viruses (norovirus, rotavirus) and the genus Enterovirus (poliovirus, coxsackievirus, echovirus).
  • Norovirus has a low infectious dose, is shed before symptoms and for weeks after, and persists on surfaces for days.

A redraw of the lecture figure. Each route is a separate link that a different measure can break.

Module 4 · Section 2

Genomes and polymerases

The genome decides what the virion has to bring with it. A virus whose RNA can be read immediately by the cell needs no polymerase in the particle. One whose genome the cell cannot read must carry the enzyme.

Key points

  • Norovirus (Caliciviridae) has positive-sense single-stranded RNA and no polymerase in the virion.
  • Rotavirus (Reoviridae) has double-stranded RNA in 11 segments and carries a polymerase in the virion.
  • Picornaviruses have positive-sense single-stranded RNA. The genus Enterovirus is a subset: poliovirus, coxsackievirus, and echovirus.

Module 4 · Section 3

Norovirus outbreak commander

Norovirus is the textbook outbreak virus. A very small dose causes illness, people shed it before they feel sick and for weeks after, and the virus persists on surfaces for days. It overtook rotavirus as a cause of gastroenteritis in U.S. children under 5 once rotavirus vaccination began.

Key points

  • Onset 12–48 hours after exposure. Stool PCR identifies the virus. Treatment is supportive fluids.
  • Wash with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. Stay home for 48 hours after symptoms stop. There is no vaccine.
  • The main complication of gastroenteritis is dehydration, and antibiotics do not help a viral infection.

Three routes, one shared feature: virus that leaves the body in vomit or stool and ends up on hands and surfaces.

Module 5 · Section 1

Norovirus vs. rotavirus

Norovirus and rotavirus are the two gastroenteritis viruses in this lecture. They share a route and a complication, and they differ in nearly everything else that a pharmacist would be asked about.

Key points

  • Both are nonenveloped, spread fecal-orally, infect the small-intestine epithelium, and are treated with supportive fluids.
  • Norovirus: positive-sense RNA, no polymerase in the virion, onset 12–48 hours, no vaccine. Rotavirus: 11-segment dsRNA, polymerase in the virion, incubation 1–3 days, children 6–24 months, oral vaccines.
  • Rotavirus: fever and vomiting ease on day 2, and diarrhea lasts 5–7 days. Hygiene alone is not enough to control it.

The rotavirus virion carries a polymerase because its double-stranded RNA cannot be read by host enzymes.

Module 5 · Section 2

The gut fluid balance

Gastroenteritis kills by dehydration, not by the virus itself. Two separate things can go wrong at the same time: the damaged lining absorbs less, and the intestine secretes more. Adjusting each one shows why the stool becomes so watery.

Key points

  • The virus infects small-intestine epithelium, destroys cells, and disrupts absorption. The main complication is dehydration.
  • Osmotic diarrhea: unabsorbed nutrients hold water in the lumen. Secretory diarrhea: the intestine actively secretes fluid. They are distinct and can occur together.
  • Treatment: oral rehydration solution, IV fluids for severe dehydration, and zinc for 10–14 days (WHO). Antibiotics do not help.

Module 5 · Section 3

Rotavirus vaccine screening

Hygiene is not enough to control rotavirus, which is why two oral, live vaccines exist. The schedule has strict age limits, so screening an infant is a matter of checking age, doses so far, and the two contraindications.

Key points

  • Rotarix: 2 doses (2 and 4 months). RotaTeq: 3 doses (2, 4, and 6 months). Both are oral.
  • The first dose must be given by 14 weeks 6 days, and the last by 8 months 0 days.
  • Contraindications: SCID and a history of intussusception. The Rotarix applicator contains latex.

Module 6 · Section 1

Poliovirus: four stages

Poliovirus enters through the mouth and begins in the gut, but whether it stays there depends on how far it gets. Most infections stop early. A small fraction reaches the nervous system, where the damage is permanent.

Key points

  • Four stages: alimentary, lymphatic, viremic, then neurologic. Most infections stop at the viremic stage.
  • About 1% of infections in non-immunized people reach the nervous system and cause flaccid paralysis.
  • IPV is injected and gives serum antibody with little gut effect. OPV gives serum antibody and gut IgA, with vaccine-associated paralysis in about 1 per 2–4 million doses.

Module 6 · Section 2

Coxsackievirus and echovirus

The coxsackieviruses and echoviruses are enteroviruses that spread fecal-orally but cause disease elsewhere. The useful skill is to link a syndrome to a tissue, and to know when a clue does not point to a single virus.

Key points

  • Coxsackie A: herpangina, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, and aseptic meningitis (skin and mucous membranes).
  • Coxsackie B: pleurodynia, myocarditis and pericarditis, and severe neonatal myocarditis (heart, pleura, pancreas, liver).
  • Echovirus: aseptic meningitis, febrile illness, and respiratory illness.

Module 6 · Section 3

Local vs. systemic infection

This is the integrating section. Every virus in the lecture enters somewhere, stays local or travels, and injures a particular tissue. Seeing them on one body shows why a gut virus, a respiratory virus, and a placental virus produce such different diseases.

Key points

  • Local infections stay at the entry site: rotavirus and norovirus in the small intestine.
  • Systemic infections spread through blood or lymph: measles (respiratory tract to skin), rubella (also the placenta), B19 (bone marrow), mumps (glands and other organs), and poliovirus (gut to the nervous system).
  • The tissue the virus reaches, not the portal of entry, decides the symptoms.
