Big ideas

Drug classes

Class cards

Penicillins

amoxicillin, ampicillin, piperacillin–tazobactam

Cephalosporins

cephalexin, cefazolin, ceftriaxone. Later generations widen activity against Gram-negative rods.

Carbapenems

meropenem, ertapenem, imipenem–cilastatin. Drugs of last resort, reserved for hospital use.

Monobactam

PHARMACIST'S LENS

Imipenem is given with cilastatin. Cilastatin has no antibacterial job: it inhibits a kidney enzyme, dehydropeptidase, that would otherwise inactivate imipenem. A combination product can protect a drug from the host as well as from the bacterium.

CAN YOU EXPLAIN IT?

A β-lactam is effective against a culture in the lab, yet an organism carrying a β-lactamase survives. Explain what the enzyme changes, and why adding an inhibitor can help.

Your response stays on your device and is not scored.

Model answer. The β-lactamase cleaves the β-lactam ring, so the drug is destroyed before it can bind a PBP. An inhibitor has no antibiotic activity of its own: it occupies or disables the enzyme, so the partner β-lactam survives long enough to block cross-linking. Inhibitors do not work against metallo-β-lactamases.

Inhibitor combinations in practice

Each inhibitor is paired with a β-lactam partner that it protects from breakdown.

Amoxicillin–clavulanate

Oral. Extends amoxicillin's coverage to many β-lactamase-producing organisms.

Piperacillin–tazobactam

IV. Broad coverage, including many Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms.

Ceftazidime–avibactam

IV. Covers many organisms that make serine carbapenemases (for example KPC), but not metallo-β-lactamase producers.

Module 2 · Section 3

Vancomycin and the membrane

Two different ways to break the envelope: block the wall's assembly (vancomycin, cycloserine) or disrupt the membrane itself (polymyxins, daptomycin). Destroying the envelope does not always mean targeting peptidoglycan.

Compare and contrast

  • Vancomycin is bactericidal and acts against Gram-positive bacteria. The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria is impermeable to large glycopeptides.
  • Polymyxins behave like a cationic detergent on the phospholipid bilayer and have poor bacterial selectivity.
  • Daptomycin is a cyclic lipopeptide that disrupts the membrane of Gram-positive cocci.
Hands-on · Match the mechanism

Which drug does each description fit?

Choose one drug for each row, then check. Watch for pairs of drugs that sound similar but act on different structures.

PHARMACIST'S LENS

Vancomycin has very low oral bioavailability, so it is given by slow intravenous infusion for systemic infection. Infusing it too quickly can cause red man syndrome. Oral vancomycin is reserved for intestinal infections, where the drug needs to stay in the gut.

CHECKPOINT

Module 3 · Section 3

Activated by the enemy

Some antibacterial drugs are inactive until a microbe converts them into their active form. That gives them a built-in selectivity, and it hands the bacterium an unusual escape route: break the activator.

From the lecture

  • Metronidazole is a prodrug that is reductively activated in anaerobic and microaerophilic organisms. The activated radical breaks DNA strands.
  • Nitrofurantoin binds DNA. Bacteria contain more of its reduced form than human cells do, which explains its selectivity.
  • The TB drugs isoniazid, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide each attack a different part of the mycobacterial envelope or metabolism.

Module 5 · Section 1

Four routes to resistance

Every resistance mechanism you have met so far falls into one of four routes: inactivate the drug, change the target, reduce entry, or pump the drug out. A single organism can combine more than one. Learn the routes and every new mechanism becomes a classification problem.

Drug inactivation

The drug is destroyed or chemically modified.

Target change

The bacterium changes what the antibiotic recognizes.

Reduced entry

Not enough drug gets in.

Efflux

The drug is pumped back out.

CAN YOU EXPLAIN IT?

Explain what attaching a carrier protein to a polysaccharide changes about the immune response, and why that matters in infants.

Your response stays on your device and is not scored.

Model answer. A plain polysaccharide triggers a T-independent B-cell response: weaker, shorter-lived, and with little memory, and infants respond poorly to it.
