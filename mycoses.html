Meet the fungi

Fungi are not bacteria with a different name. They are eukaryotes with their own wall, their own membrane sterol, and their own ways of changing shape. This module shows what makes a fungus a fungus, why some people are far more vulnerable than others, and how the laboratory finds it.

Module 1 · Section 1

Anatomy of a fungal cell

A fungal cell is built like a human cell on the inside and like nothing in the human body on the outside. That outside, a wall and a different membrane sterol, is where antifungal drugs find their targets.

Key points

  • Fungi are eukaryotes, with a nucleus, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum. A fungal cell is about 4 µm across, compared with about 1 µm for a typical bacterium.
  • The fungal cell wall is built from chitin (a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine) and β-glucan. Bacteria use peptidoglycan, and human cells have no wall.
  • The fungal membrane contains ergosterol. Human membranes contain cholesterol, and bacterial membranes contain no sterols.
  • Fungi make sexual and asexual spores, not endospores. They need organic carbon, and none are obligate anaerobes.

The fungal cell looks like the human cell inside (nucleus, mitochondria) and unlike it on the outside (a wall and a different sterol). The bacterium differs in both places.

Module 1 · Section 2

Yeast, mold, or dimorphic?

A fungus can live as a yeast, as a mold, or as both. Temperature decides which form the dimorphic fungi take, and the form you see under the microscope is a clue to the organism.

Key points

  • Yeasts are single cells that reproduce by budding. Candida and Cryptococcus are yeasts.
  • Molds grow as branching filaments called hyphae. Aspergillus, Mucor, and the dermatophytes are molds.
  • Thermally dimorphic fungi are molds at room temperature and yeasts at 37 °C. Histoplasma, Blastomyces, and Sporothrix follow this pattern.
  • Coccidioides is the odd one: at 37 °C it forms a spherule full of endospores rather than a budding yeast.

Candida and Cryptococcus are yeasts. Aspergillus, Mucor, and the dermatophytes are molds. Coccidioides is a mold in the soil and a spherule in tissue.

Module 1 · Section 3

The host defense challenge

Healthy people inhale fungal spores every day without harm. Disease happens when a specific defense is missing, and each defect opens the door to a predictable set of fungi.

Key points

  • Inhalation is the usual portal of entry. Candida is the exception, because it is already part of the normal flora.
  • Fungi make no endotoxin or exotoxin. Tissue damage comes from the host response, which is granulomatous (macrophages, giant cells, lymphocytes) or suppurative (neutrophils).
  • The defect predicts the fungus: neutropenia points to Candida and Aspergillus, T-cell loss to Cryptococcus, and ketoacidosis to Mucor and Rhizopus.
  • Lost barriers and lost flora, from catheters and antibiotics, point to Candida.

Module 1 · Section 4

The fungal investigation lab

A fungal infection is found with the right test on the right specimen. Fungi can also harm without infecting, through toxins and allergy, so the first question is what kind of harm is happening.

Key points

  • KOH dissolves human cells to reveal fungal hyphae. India ink outlines the capsule of Cryptococcus.
  • Culture on Sabouraud agar identifies the organism, and cultures of dimorphic fungi held at 20 °C show the mold form.
  • Serology (Coccidioides IgM and IgG), the Histoplasma urine antigen, PCR, and MALDI-TOF round out the toolkit.
  • Fungal harm has three forms: mycosis (infection), mycotoxicosis (poisoning by a fungal toxin), and allergy. Amanita α-amanitin, ergot, and aflatoxin are the toxin examples.

Module 2 · Section 1

Destroying the cell wall

The echinocandins attack a structure that humans do not have. They are the newest first-line class for invasive Candida, and their mechanism explains both their strengths and their limits.

Key points

  • Echinocandins (caspofungin, anidulafungin, micafungin) inhibit β-glucan synthase, so the wall weakens and the fungus lyses.
  • None of the echinocandins is orally active, so they are given by IV infusion.
  • They cause few CYP-mediated interactions, because they do not inhibit CYP and are poor CYP substrates. That is fewer than the azoles.
  • Antibacterial drugs are ineffective against fungi. A penicillin targets peptidoglycan, which a fungal wall does not contain.

Module 2 · Section 2

Disrupting the membrane

Amphotericin B is old, effective, and hard on the kidneys. Its mechanism explains why it works on fungi and why a different packaging of the same molecule protects the patient.

Key points

  • Polyenes (amphotericin B, nystatin, natamycin) have a hydrophilic sugar region and a hydrophobic macrocycle with 4 to 7 conjugated double bonds.
  • Current evidence says amphotericin B aggregates extract ergosterol from the membrane (a sterol sponge). Pores and ion leakage are secondary. Binding to ergosterol is preferred over binding to cholesterol.
  • Amphotericin B is the only polyene given IV. Its toxicities are nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions, and the liposomal form limits renal toxicity.
  • Oral nystatin is not absorbed from the gut, so it has no systemic risk. Natamycin is used only for infections of the eye.

A polyene has two faces. The number of conjugated double bonds tracks both antifungal activity and toxicity.

Module 2 · Section 3

Blocking ergosterol synthesis

Two classes shut down the same factory at different steps. Seeing where each block sits tells you what accumulates, which drug is oral, and why the azoles interact with so many other medicines.

Key points

  • Azoles block CYP450 14α-demethylase (CYP51), the enzyme that removes the 14α-methyl group from lanosterol. The azole nitrogen binds the iron of the heme.
  • Allylamines (terbinafine, naftifine, butenafine) inhibit squalene epoxidase, an earlier step. Squalene builds up and is toxic to the cell. Tolnaftate has a different structure but the same mechanism.
  • Imidazoles (ketoconazole, clotrimazole) are used mostly on the skin. Triazoles (fluconazole, itraconazole) are preferred orally. Mold-active azoles are voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazole.
  • Because azoles also inhibit many human CYP450 isoforms, they have numerous drug-drug interactions.

Module 2 · Section 4

Beyond the membrane

Two drugs reach inside the cell: one corrupts DNA synthesis and one blocks cell division. Matching every class to its mechanism is the skill that lets you reason about a new patient.

Key points

  • Flucytosine is taken up by cytosine permease and converted by cytosine deaminase to fluorouracil. Fluorouracil inhibits thymidylate synthase. Human cells lack cytosine deaminase.
  • Flucytosine is used with amphotericin B, because resistance develops quickly when it is used alone. Its main adverse reaction is bone marrow toxicity.
  • Griseofulvin binds tubulin and blocks the mitotic spindle. It is used orally for cutaneous infections that do not respond to topical agents, and it is ineffective topically.
  • Terbinafine is lipophilic and distributes to hair, skin, nails, and fat after oral dosing, which makes it useful for nail infections.

Module 3 · Section 1

Classify the mycosis

The lecture groups mycoses into four categories. Two of them describe where the fungus lives. Two describe how it got there or who it infects. Mixing those up is the most common classification slip.

Key points

  • Cutaneous: infection of superficial keratinized structures only (skin, hair, nails).
  • Subcutaneous: trauma introduces the fungus into deeper tissue.
  • Systemic: typically follows inhalation of spores.
  • Opportunistic: nonpathogenic or weakly pathogenic fungi that cause disease mainly in immunocompromised patients.

Depth is one idea: how far the fungus gets. Host status is a separate idea. An opportunistic fungus is defined by who it infects, not by where.

Module 3 · Section 2

Dermatophyte detective

Dermatophytes eat keratin, so they stay where keratin is. That single fact explains the sites they infect, the test you use, and why some cases need a pill instead of a cream.

Key points

  • Dermatophytes (Trichophyton, Epidermophyton, Microsporum) infect skin, hair, and nails but not deeper tissue.
  • Tinea names the body part: capitis (head), corporis (body), cruris (groin), pedis (foot). Onychomycosis is a nail infection.
  • Diagnosis: KOH preparation of scrapings shows septate hyphae; culture uses Sabouraud agar.
  • Topical agents (terbinafine, clotrimazole, tolnaftate, undecylenic acid) treat skin. Hair and nail infections need oral griseofulvin, itraconazole, or terbinafine.

Module 3 · Section 3

The rose gardener’s case

Sporothrix is a soil and plant mold that turns into a yeast at body temperature. A small puncture is enough to bring it under the skin.

Key points

  • Sporotrichosis is a subcutaneous mycosis caused by the dimorphic fungus Sporothrix schenckii.
  • Entry is by penetrating injury, such as a rose thorn. Lesions are a local pustule or ulcer with nodules, usually painless.
  • Tissue shows cigar-shaped yeasts. Culture at 20 °C shows hyphae with daisy-like conidia.
  • Treatment is itraconazole or terbinafine. Prevention is skin protection.

Module 4 · Section 1

Endemic fungal investigation

These four fungi share a soil habitat, an inhaled spore, and a temperature switch. They differ in the shape they take in tissue and in where they live. That shape and place are your fastest clues.

Key points

  • All four are dimorphic: a mold in soil that becomes a yeast (or a spherule for Coccidioides) in the lung.
  • Portal of entry is inhalation. At 20 °C each grows as a mold in the laboratory.
  • Tissue forms: Coccidioides, spherules; Histoplasma, small yeasts inside macrophages; Blastomyces, yeast with one broad-based bud; Paracoccidioides, yeast with multiple buds.
  • Ranges are expanding, so geography is a clue and not a rule.

Module 4 · Section 2

The Coccidioides life cycle

Coccidioides is the only endemic fungus in this lecture that makes a spherule. Knowing the cycle explains why the infection can multiply in tissue and why immunity decides the outcome.

Key points

  • Arthrospores form in soil, are inhaled, and become spherules in the lung.
  • A spherule fills with endospores. When the wall ruptures, endospores are released and form new spherules.
  • Just over half of infections are asymptomatic or minimal. About 1% disseminate to meninges, bone, or skin.
  • Cell-mediated immunity forms granulomas that wall off the fungus. Dissemination occurs when that immunity is inadequate.

Module 4 · Section 3

Systemic mycosis case files

Treatment follows organism and severity. Most endemic mycoses begin with an azole, and the question is when the situation is severe enough to move to amphotericin B.

Key points

  • Histoplasma, Blastomyces, and Paracoccidioides: itraconazole is the usual first choice for mild to moderate disease.
  • Coccidioides: fluconazole or itraconazole for persistent infection or mild dissemination. Meningitis: fluconazole.
  • Severe disease of any of the four: amphotericin B.
  • Asymptomatic or mild primary coccidioidomycosis often needs no treatment, but higher-risk patients are treated. There is no vaccine.

Module 5 · Section 1

From colonization to infection

Candida albicans is part of the normal flora. The lecture’s rule is simple: intact skin and cell-mediated immunity prevent overgrowth. This lab lets you break each protection and see what follows.

Key points

  • Candida is normal flora. Intact skin and cell-mediated immunity prevent overgrowth.
  • Invasion involves the yeast-to-hypha switch and biofilm formation on catheters.
  • C. albicans causes thrush, vaginitis, esophagitis, diaper rash, and chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis.
  • It can disseminate to cause endocarditis, candidemia, and endophthalmitis, and it can infect IV and urinary catheters.

Module 5 · Section 2

Localized or invasive?

Treatment follows the site and the severity. A rash and a bloodstream infection are the same organism but belong in different parts of the formulary.

Key points

  • Mucosal and skin disease: topical clotrimazole, nystatin, or miconazole.
  • Moderate to severe thrush, esophageal disease, and vaginitis (as an option): fluconazole.
  • Candidemia: an echinocandin first, with removal or replacement of the central venous catheter.
  • Prevention: fluconazole for high-risk infants under 1,000 g in nurseries with a high rate of invasive candidiasis. No vaccine exists.

The species name changes the plan. A fluconazole step-down that is safe for C. albicans can fail for C. krusei, so identification and susceptibility results are part of the treatment decision.

Key points

Module 6 · Section 1

Cryptococcus and the CNS

Cryptococcus enters by inhalation and spreads to the brain when cell-mediated immunity is weak. The order of treatment is the lesson: kill the yeast first, then restore immunity.

Key points

  • Cryptococcus neoformans is a yeast with a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis. India ink shows the capsule.
  • It is a leading cause of death in advanced HIV disease. Disease occurs with compromised cell-mediated immunity and spreads to the CNS.
  • Induction: liposomal amphotericin B plus flucytosine. Then fluconazole for consolidation and suppression. Echinocandins are inactive.
  • ART is started about 4 to 6 weeks after antifungal therapy begins, because earlier ART risks IRIS, which may be deadly.

The halo on India ink is the capsule. Mucicarmine stains the capsule red in tissue.

Module 6 · Section 2

The many faces of Aspergillus

One organism gives three diseases, and the host decides which. The same inhaled spore can sit quietly in a cavity, trigger an allergic response, or invade a vessel.

Key points

  • Aspergillosis is commonly caused by Aspergillus fumigatus or A. flavus. It is acquired by inhaling conidia.
  • Forms: invasive disease, aspergilloma (fungus ball), and allergic disease (ABPA).
  • Risk: lung disease or weak immunity such as neutropenia or high-dose steroids. Hyphae can invade blood vessels and disseminate.
  • Invasive disease: voriconazole; alternatives are isavuconazole or liposomal amphotericin B. Posaconazole or voriconazole can be used for prophylaxis in high-risk patients.

Module 6 · Section 3

The immunocompromised patient

The host defect is often the best clue to the fungus. This sorting task asks you to read the patient first and the microscope second.

Key points

  • Neutropenia and high-dose steroids favor Aspergillus and Candida.
  • Advanced HIV and weak cell-mediated immunity favor Cryptococcus and mucosal Candida.
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis favors Mucor and Rhizopus, which are molds with nonseptate hyphae that invade blood vessels.
