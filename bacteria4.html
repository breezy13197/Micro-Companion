Module 1

Mycobacteria: built to persist

Mycobacterium tuberculosis breaks the usual staining rules, grows on its own timetable, and demands four drugs instead of one. Every one of those facts traces back to a single unusual structure: its cell wall.

  • Differentiate M. tuberculosis, MAC, and M. leprae by host, transmission, and treatment.
In this module
Module 1 · Section 1

The wall that won’t wash out

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is neither Gram-positive nor Gram-negative. Instead, it’s identified by a stain that tests its cell wall directly — and that same wall explains almost everything else about how this organism behaves.

Key points

  • Acid-fast: the cell wall’s lipid content (~60%) retains carbol fuchsin stain even after an acid-alcohol wash.
  • The wall is rich in mycolic acid, a long-chain fatty acid responsible for acid-fastness and for resistance to acids, bases, and drying.
  • M. tuberculosis is an obligate aerobe with a doubling time of about 18 hours — compare E. coli or S. aureus at roughly 20 minutes.
  • Virulence factors tied to this wall include cord factor (trehalose dimycolate), phthiocerol dimycocerosate, tuberculosis necrotizing toxin (TNT), and ESAT-6.

The mycobacterial cell envelope, from the cytoplasm to the outermost mycolic acids.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Module 1 · Section 2

Infection, containment, and disease

A single inhaled droplet can end in very different places. Which path a patient takes depends entirely on how well the immune system holds the line.

Key points

  • Airborne droplet nuclei reach the alveoli, where bacilli multiply inside alveolar macrophages.
  • Adequate containment forms a granuloma and produces latent TB infection: no symptoms, no transmission.
  • Inadequate containment allows active TB disease, which may be transmitted via respiratory droplets.
  • Only about 10% of latent infections progress to active disease — but reactivation can occur years later if immune control wanes.

How a single exposure can lead to latency, active disease, or later reactivation.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A patient with latent TB infection is not sick and cannot transmit the organism — but they are also not cured. That distinction is why preventive therapy exists as its own clinical decision, separate from treating active disease.

Module 1 · Section 3

Why four drugs?

Active TB is never treated with a single drug. Each of the four first-line agents hits a different target, which is exactly what keeps a mutant resistant to one drug from taking over the population.

Key points

  • First-line drugs: isoniazid (INH), rifampin (RIF), pyrazinamide (PZA), and ethambutol (EMB).
  • The initial phase uses all four for 2 months; the continuation phase uses INH and RIF alone for 4 months.
  • Streptomycin can substitute for EMB in some countries, but is not considered first-line in the U.S.
  • Resistance to both INH and RIF defines MDR-TB; adding resistance to a fluoroquinolone and a Group A drug (bedaquiline or linezolid) defines XDR-TB.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Monotherapy is never appropriate for active TB: a large bacillary population almost always contains a few organisms already resistant to any single drug, and treating with one agent selects for exactly those survivors.

Module 1 · Section 4

Resistance lab

Resistance in M. tuberculosis never arrives on a plasmid — this organism has none. Every resistant strain got that way through its own chromosomal mutation, which means the target a drug hits and the target a mutation breaks are often the very same gene.

Key points

  • Resistance originates from chromosomal mutations, not plasmid acquisition — M. tuberculosis has no plasmids.
  • A mutation in catalase-peroxidase (KatG) blocks the enzyme isoniazid needs for activation, conferring isoniazid resistance.
  • A mutation in a mycolic-acid-synthesis gene can also reduce isoniazid susceptibility, since that pathway is the drug’s direct target.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Susceptibility testing matters before finalizing a regimen for exactly this reason: a strain can look identical on a smear while carrying a mutation that silently defeats one of the four first-line drugs.

Module 1 · Section 5

TB, MAC, or leprosy?

Three species share the genus Mycobacterium and very little else. Host immune status, growth pattern, and drug susceptibility are what actually tell them apart at the bedside.

Key points

  • M. tuberculosis: causes classic pulmonary TB across a range of immune status; treated with standard four-drug therapy.
  • MAC (M. avium-intracellulare complex): the most common atypical mycobacterium; mainly affects patients with CD4 counts under 200 cells/µL; highly resistant to standard anti-TB drugs.
  • M. leprae: causes leprosy; obligate intracellular with a 12–14 day doubling time; treated with rifampin, dapsone, and clofazimine.

Structural outliers: filaments and no wall

Actinomyces, Nocardia, and Mycoplasma are the organisms students most often confuse, because none of them fit the standard bacterial picture. Two look almost identical under the microscope but demand opposite treatment; the third has no cell wall at all.

In this module
Module 2 · Section 1

Actinomyces or Nocardia?

Under the microscope, these two organisms look almost the same — both form branching filaments that resemble fungal hyphae. Almost everything else about them is opposite.

Key points

  • Both form branching, Gram-positive filaments; some strains are weakly acid-fast.
  • Actinomyces israelii is anaerobic and lives as normal oral flora; disease follows mucosal trauma.
  • Nocardia asteroides is aerobic and lives in soil; disease follows inhalation.
  • Actinomycosis: a draining sinus tract with “sulfur granules” in the pus, treated with penicillin G. Nocardiosis: pneumonia that can spread from lung to brain, treated with TMP-SMX.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Module 2 · Section 2

Follow the infection

Same branching filaments, opposite starting point. One organism begins inside the patient; the other arrives from the outside world.

Key points

  • Actinomycosis begins with disruption of the oral mucosa — dental trauma, extraction, poor dentition.
  • Nocardiosis begins with inhalation of an environmental soil organism.
  • Nocardia characteristically disseminates from the lung to the brain.
  • Nocardiosis disproportionately affects immunocompromised patients; actinomycosis can occur in otherwise healthy people after trauma.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A patient’s exposure history often does more diagnostic work here than any lab test — recent dental work points one way, gardening or construction dust the other.

Module 2 · Section 3

Remove the cell wall

Every bacterium you’ve met so far has had a cell wall of some kind. Mycoplasma pneumoniae doesn’t — and that single missing structure explains nearly everything unusual about it.

Key points

  • Mycoplasma pneumoniae is the smallest free-living organism and lacks a cell wall entirely.
  • Its membrane contains cholesterol — the only bacterial membrane known to do so.
  • It is termed “atypical” because it resists routine culture and its pneumonia doesn’t resemble the classic pneumococcal picture.
  • Transmission is by respiratory droplet.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Seeing a beta-lactam ordered for suspected atypical pneumonia should be an automatic pause: without a wall, there is no target for that entire drug class to hit.

Module 2 · Section 4

Walking pneumonia case

Real diagnosis rarely arrives as a single fact. Clues build one at a time — decide what you think after each one, then see whether the next clue changes your mind.

Key points

  • M. pneumoniae attaches to respiratory epithelium and inhibits ciliary motion, causing necrosis, rather than invading cells directly.
  • A classic clue: imaging that looks worse than the patient does clinically.
  • First-line treatment is a macrolide; tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are alternatives.
  • Crowded living situations favor droplet spread between people.
Hands-on

Spirochetes: same shape, very different disease

Treponema pallidum and Borrelia burgdorferi are built from the same unusual blueprint — a thin, flexible, motile rod. From there, their exposure routes, disease courses, and treatments diverge almost completely.

In this module
Module 3 · Section 1

Build a spirochete

Treponema pallidum and Borrelia burgdorferi look almost nothing alike in the diseases they cause, but structurally they’re built from the same unusual blueprint: a thin, flexible, motile rod wound around its own internal flagella.

Key points

  • Spirochetes are thin-walled, flexible, motile rods.
  • A blue outer membrane encloses the periplasmic space.
  • Periplasmic (endo)flagella wrap around the cell cylinder and anchor at both poles, producing the organism’s corkscrew motion.
  • A thin peptidoglycan layer supports the cell cylinder from inside the outer membrane.

A spirochete’s coiled body: outer membrane, periplasmic flagella, and inner cell cylinder.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Module 3 · Section 2

Stage the syphilis patient

Syphilis unfolds in stages, and the stage a patient is in determines what you’d expect to find on exam — and sometimes which regimen applies.

Key points

  • Primary syphilis: a painless chancre at the site of inoculation, which may heal spontaneously even as the organism spreads through the bloodstream.
  • Secondary syphilis: systemic disease — fever, malaise, weight loss, and a maculopapular rash classically involving the palms and soles.
  • Tertiary syphilis: CNS involvement and cardiovascular lesions, occurring after a period of latency.
  • Congenital syphilis (vertical transmission): skin and bone lesions, hepatosplenomegaly, and stillbirth.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A chancre that heals on its own is easy to mistake for resolution. It isn’t — the organism is already spreading through the bloodstream, which is exactly why serologic screening, not symptom-watching, drives syphilis diagnosis.

Module 3 · Section 3

Follow Lyme disease

Lyme disease follows its own three-stage course, and unlike syphilis, prompt treatment can stop the pathway before it ever reaches the later stages.

Key points

  • Lyme disease is the most common vector-borne illness in the United States, transmitted by tick exposure.
  • Stage 1: erythema migrans, the expanding “bullseye” rash with central clearing.
  • Stage 2: neurologic and cardiac involvement, including myocarditis and meningitis.
  • Stage 3: arthritis and encephalopathy.

Module 3 · Section 4

Syphilis or Lyme?

Both are spirochetes. Both cause a stereotyped skin finding early on. From there, almost everything about exposure, transmission, and treatment diverges.

Key points

  • T. pallidum spreads by sexual contact and vertical (perinatal) transmission; humans are the only reservoir.
  • B. burgdorferi spreads by tick bite — a zoonotic infection, never person-to-person.
  • Syphilis’s early lesion is a painless chancre; Lyme’s early lesion is an expanding bullseye rash.
  • First-line therapy differs: benzathine penicillin G for syphilis; doxycycline or amoxicillin for Lyme.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Exposure history is often the fastest way to tell these two apart at the bedside: a sexual history points toward syphilis, an outdoor or tick history points toward Lyme — well before any lab result comes back.

Module 3 · Section 5

Reaction detective

Treating a spirochetal infection can trigger a reaction that looks alarming but isn’t an allergy at all. Telling it apart from a true drug allergy, or from anaphylaxis, changes what you do next.

Key points

  • The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction follows antibiotic treatment of a high-spirochete-burden infection (syphilis, Lyme), from lysis of the organisms releasing endotoxin-like substances.
  • Onset is 24–72 hours after the first dose; more than half of treated patients are affected to some degree.
  • Management is supportive (antipyretics, IV fluids) — it is not an allergy and does not require stopping the antibiotic.
  • True allergy/intolerance and anaphylaxis have different onset patterns and treatments; anaphylaxis is a medical emergency.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Reflexively stopping the antibiotic at the first sign of fever can do more harm than good here — recognizing Jarisch-Herxheimer for what it is keeps the patient on effective therapy.

Chlamydiae: one organism, two cellular identities

Chlamydia doesn’t just have an unusual structure — it has two of them. Understanding why it switches between an infectious form and a replicating form explains its diagnosis, its treatment, and why it can’t be grown like an ordinary bacterium.

Pharmacist’s questionIf a single organism spends part of its life unable to infect and part of its life unable to replicate, what does that mean for how you interrupt it?

In this module
Module 4 · Section 1

EB or RB?

Chlamydia lives a double life: one form built to travel and infect, another built to stay put and multiply. Neither form can do the other’s job.

Key points

  • Chlamydiae are obligate intracellular organisms — they cannot grow independently and depend on the host cell for energy.
  • The elementary body (EB) is the infectious, extracellular form: metabolically inactive, but resistant to environmental stress.
  • The reticulate body (RB) is the non-infectious, intracellular form: metabolically active, replicating by binary fission.
  • The cell wall is rigid but lacks typical peptidoglycan — one more way this organism breaks the usual bacterial pattern.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Because Chlamydia depends entirely on the host cell for energy, it can’t be cultured the way most bacteria can — which is exactly why diagnosis leans on inclusion staining, serology, or nucleic acid amplification instead of a standard culture plate.

Module 4 · Section 2

Build the chlamydial lifecycle

Five steps, always in the same order, always as a loop. Miss one, and the cycle can’t continue to the next.

Key points

  • Attachment: the infectious EB contacts an epithelial cell.
  • Entry: the EB enters the cell within a membrane-bound inclusion.
  • Replication: inside the inclusion, the organism converts to RBs, which divide repeatedly.
  • Differentiation: RBs convert back into infectious EBs.
  • Release: EBs leave the cell by lysis or extrusion, ready to infect new cells.

The chlamydial developmental cycle repeats as a loop, one infected cell at a time.

Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

This cycle takes roughly 48–72 hours to complete, which is part of why a short antibiotic course can fail to fully clear infection — treatment needs to cover the whole developmental window, not just a symptomatic snapshot of it.

Module 4 · Section 3

Break the lifecycle

Every stage of this cycle depends on the one before it. Predict what happens when a single step fails, before you see the answer.

Key points

  • Only the EB is infectious; only the RB replicates.
  • Blocking differentiation leaves RBs behind with no way to become infectious.
  • Blocking replication caps the number of organisms produced inside the inclusion.
  • Blocking release keeps the infection confined to the one cell it already occupies.

Module 4 · Section 4

Which Chlamydia?

Three species, three very different exposure stories — even though the organism, the lifecycle, and the drug of choice are largely the same.

Key points

  • C. trachomatis: humans only; sexual or perinatal transmission; urethritis, PID, conjunctivitis, trachoma.
  • C. pneumoniae: humans only; respiratory droplets; atypical pneumonia, bronchitis, sinusitis.
  • C. psittaci: birds are the natural host; inhaled dried droppings or secretions; psittacosis (“parrot fever”).
  • All three are treated with doxycycline; C. pneumoniae also responds to macrolides.

Module 4 · Section 5

C. trachomatis clinical challenge

Most C. trachomatis infections never announce themselves. Walk this case from a routine screen through the treatment decision that follows.

Key points

  • Asymptomatic carriers are an important reservoir for ongoing transmission.
  • Non-gonococcal urethritis classically presents with a watery, nonpurulent discharge.
  • Roughly 10–30% of C. trachomatis infections are coinfected with Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
  • First-line treatment for nonpregnant patients is doxycycline 100 mg BID for 7 days, per current CDC guidance.

Rickettsiae: vessels, vectors, and the outlier

In this module
Module 5 · Section 1

From endothelium to rash

Almost every finding in Rocky Mountain spotted fever traces back to one starting point: a cell type most infections leave alone.

Key points

  • Rickettsiae are obligate intracellular organisms, difficult to see by light microscopy.
  • Rickettsia rickettsii invades endothelial cells lining small blood vessels.
  • Phospholipase D and a pore-forming hemolysin are believed to help the organism enter cells and escape the phagosome.
  • Vessel damage (vasculitis) increases capillary permeability, producing the rash, headache, and edema that follow.

One root cause, four downstream effects: how vessel damage becomes systemic illness.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Module 5 · Section 2

RMSF clinical clock

This is one of the few infections in this companion where waiting for confirmation can cost a patient’s life. Walk a case forward and decide when treatment should start.

Key points

  • Acute onset: fever, severe headache, myalgia, prostration.
  • The rash follows fever onset by 2-6 days — treatment should not wait for it.
  • Untreated disease can progress to CNS changes, disseminated intravascular coagulation, edema, and circulatory collapse.
  • Doxycycline is the treatment of choice, and no vaccine is available; prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended.

Module 5 · Section 3

Vector match

Four organisms, four exposure stories. The vector — or lack of one — is often the fastest way to tell them apart.

Key points

  • Rickettsia rickettsii: Dermacentor tick; Rocky Mountain spotted fever.
  • Ehrlichia chaffeensis: lone star tick (Amblyomma americanum); human monocytropic ehrlichiosis.
  • Anaplasma phagocytophilum: blacklegged tick (Ixodes scapularis) — the same tick that carries Lyme disease; human granulocytic anaplasmosis.
  • Coxiella burnetii: not tick-borne in humans; livestock-associated aerosols or unpasteurized dairy; Q fever.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A shared vector matters clinically: a patient exposed to an Ixodes tick could plausibly have Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, or both at once, which is part of why some tick-borne panels test for multiple organisms together.

Module 5 · Section 4

Find the outlier

Three of these four organisms share a family resemblance in how they spread. One doesn’t — and a second organism, barely related, breaks the pattern even further.

Key points

  • Coxiella burnetii breaks the tick pattern: it spreads through livestock-associated aerosols and unpasteurized dairy.
  • Coxiella also causes a distinct chronic disease — most often endocarditis — treated with hydroxychloroquine plus doxycycline for around 18 months.
  • A related outlier: Wolbachia, an intracellular bacterium living inside the roundworm Wuchereria bancrofti (which causes elephantiasis), is also treated with doxycycline — indirectly, by killing the bacteria the worm depends on.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

One more organism breaks the pattern even further: Wolbachia isn’t tick-borne at all. It lives inside the roundworm Wuchereria bancrofti and makes the worm’s disease worse. Doxycycline treats this infection indirectly — by killing the Wolbachia the worm depends on, not the worm itself.

Module 5 · Section 5

Shared tick problem

One tick bite, several possible organisms. Knowing which ones could plausibly share that same bite is what turns a single exposure history into a real differential.

Key points

  • Ixodes scapularis carries both Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease) and Anaplasma phagocytophilum.
  • Rickettsia rickettsii and Ehrlichia chaffeensis use different tick species entirely (Dermacentor and the lone star tick, respectively).
  • Coxiella burnetii and Chlamydia psittaci aren’t tick-borne at all.
  • Persistent symptoms despite treatment for one tick-borne illness can be a clue to a coinfection, not necessarily treatment failure.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

When a patient reports a confirmed tick bite, the tick species and geographic region narrow the differential fast — but a single bite can sometimes carry more than one organism at once, which is why persistent or atypical symptoms after treatment can warrant testing for a coinfection rather than assuming treatment failure.

Module 6 · Section 1

What doesn’t fit?

Three findings belong to an organism. One doesn’t — it belongs to a different organism from a different module that happens to sound similar. Catching the swap is the whole skill.

Key points

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis has an unusually lipid-rich wall — not no wall at all, which is Mycoplasma’s trait.
  • Treponema pallidum spreads sexually and vertically — not by a tick, which is Borrelia’s route.
  • Chlamydia trachomatis infects only humans — a bird reservoir belongs to C. psittaci instead.
  • Rickettsia rickettsii is tick-borne — unpasteurized dairy exposure belongs to Coxiella burnetii instead.

Exposure detective

One clue, one organism, as fast as you can get there. No case history, no lab values — just the exposure.

Module 6 · Section 3

Treatment constraint

Instead of asking which drug treats an organism, start from a single biological fact and reason forward to which drugs couldn’t possibly work.

Key points

  • No cell wall → beta-lactams have no target.
  • Obligate intracellular growth → routine culture won’t recover the organism.
  • No plasmids → resistance can only arise by chromosomal mutation.
