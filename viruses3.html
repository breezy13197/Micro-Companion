Module 1 · Section 1

Build a herpesvirion

Herpesviruses share one architecture, and that shared design is why one set of ideas covers HSV, VZV, EBV, and CMV. Each layer has a job, and the middle layer, the tegument, carries regulatory proteins into the cell.

Key points

  • An icosahedral capsid holds a linear double-stranded DNA genome, wrapped in a lipoprotein envelope.
  • The tegument sits between the nucleocapsid and the envelope and contains regulatory proteins.
  • Virions are large (120–200 nm; only poxviruses are larger), and replication happens in the nucleus.

Module 1 · Section 2

Latency and reactivation

HSV does not leave. After the first infection it persists in neurons, the viral DNA closes into a circle, and a few noncoding RNAs keep replication suppressed. Flip the switch and watch what each state looks like.

Key points

  • During latency, HSV DNA is circular and is not integrated into the host chromosome.
  • Latency-associated transcripts (LATs) are noncoding regulatory RNAs that suppress viral replication and early gene expression.
  • Reactivation restarts viral replication. The lecture does not name its trigger, so this activity does not guess one.

Module 1 · Section 3

Acyclovir, foscarnet, and urgent HSV

Acyclovir is selective because it needs a viral enzyme to switch on. Foscarnet skips that step. Both stop DNA synthesis, which is why neither can touch a latent genome.

Key points

  • Viral thymidine kinase adds the first phosphate to acyclovir, and cellular kinases add the next two.
  • Acyclovir triphosphate is an obligate chain terminator of viral DNA synthesis. Foscarnet blocks the viral DNA polymerase directly and needs no activation.
  • Neonatal HSV and HSV encephalitis are emergencies, so IV acyclovir starts without delay.

Module 2 · Section 1

VZV: chickenpox, zoster, and vaccines

One virus, two diseases. VZV causes chickenpox, hides in nerve tissue, and returns as zoster in a single dermatome. Zoster vesicles contain live virus, so a person with shingles can give chickenpox to someone who has never had it.

Key points

  • Chickenpox shows widespread vesicles. Zoster is local to one dermatome. There is one serotype and humans are the natural host.
  • Acyclovir treats shingles in adults and can be used in immunocompromised children, and no drug cures the latent stage.
  • Varivax and Zostavax are live attenuated. Shingrix is a recombinant envelope glycoprotein vaccine given as 2 doses, 2–6 months apart.

Module 2 · Section 2

EBV and B lymphocytes

EBV is structurally typical and antigenically unique. Its target cell, the B lymphocyte, explains both its symptoms and its persistence.

Key points

  • EBV infects B lymphocytes, spreads in saliva, and can remain latent in B cells. Most adults have antibodies.
  • Infectious mononucleosis brings fever, sore throat, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatitis, and sometimes encephalitis.
  • Uncomplicated mononucleosis needs no specific treatment, and recovery takes 2–3 weeks. No vaccine exists.

Module 2 · Section 3

CMV and the transplant patient

CMV is quiet in healthy people and dangerous when cell-mediated immunity falls. Each CMV drug acts on a different viral target, and the target explains when you use it.

Key points

  • CMV is the most common cause of infectious birth defects in the U.S., and infection is asymptomatic unless the patient is immunocompromised.
  • Ganciclovir (IV) and valganciclovir (oral prodrug) are activated by viral UL97 kinase. Foscarnet needs no activation. Acyclovir is only weakly active against CMV.
  • Letermovir blocks the terminase (DNA packaging) complex for prophylaxis in transplant recipients. Maribavir inhibits pUL97 for post-transplant CMV that does not respond to available treatment.

Module 3 · Section 1

DNA viruses: nucleus or cytoplasm?

Most DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus, where the host RNA polymerase lives. Poxviruses are the exception, and they solve the problem by bringing their own polymerase.

Key points

  • Herpesviruses and HPV replicate in the nucleus. Herpesvirus DNA is linear and HPV DNA is circular.
  • Poxviruses replicate in the cytoplasm and carry a DNA-dependent RNA polymerase because they cannot access the host enzyme.
  • Smallpox was eradicated: one stable serotype, humans as the only host, rapid protection by vaccination, recognizable disease, and no carrier state.

Module 3 · Section 3

HPV: warts, cancer, and vaccination

HPV makes two kinds of trouble. Low-risk types cause warts. High-risk types use E6 and E7 to disable p53 and pRb, the checkpoint proteins that normally stop a damaged cell from dividing.

Key points

  • HPV-6 and HPV-11 cause genital warts. HPV-16 and HPV-18 cause roughly 70% of cervical cancers.
  • Early genes E1 and E2 drive replication and transcription. E6 inactivates p53, E7 inactivates pRb, and L1 and L2 encode capsid proteins.
  • Gardasil 9 covers 9 types and prevents new infection. It does not treat existing HPV infection or disease.

Module 4 · Section 1

The influenza virion: H and N

Influenza is built to change. Eight RNA segments can be traded between viruses, and two surface proteins, hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, are the main targets of immunity.

Key points

  • Segmented negative-sense RNA, a helical nucleocapsid, an envelope, and a virion RNA polymerase that transcribes RNA into mRNA.
  • Hemagglutinin (H) binds host cell receptors. Neuraminidase (N) breaks down neuraminic acid so virions can leave, and it also helps break through mucus.
  • Each virus carries one H and one N. Type (A–D) is set by the internal ribonucleoprotein, subtype by H and N. NS-1 can inhibit interferon production.

Module 4 · Section 2

Drift and shift simulator

Drift is a slow slide. Shift is a jump. Run both and see which one keeps last year’s antibodies working and which one leaves a population defenseless.

Key points

  • Antigenic drift: minor change in the genome from gradual mutations.
  • Antigenic shift: major change from reassortment, in which entire RNA segments are exchanged.
  • Reassortment needs a segmented genome and a cell infected by two compatible viruses.

Module 4 · Section 3

Flu outbreak investigator

You have six pieces of outbreak evidence. Decide which ones describe drift, which describe shift, and which describe infection that jumped from an animal. Then explain why a fever and body aches can occur even when the virus never enters the blood.

Key points

  • The 2009 swine-origin H1N1 virus was a quadruple reassortant of North American swine, Eurasian swine, North American avian, and human viruses. Most people lacked antibodies to its hemagglutinin.
  • Avian H5 infections have occurred mostly in people with direct animal contact, and person-to-person spread is rare. Check current CDC counts, which change.
  • Influenza infects respiratory epithelium. Viremia is rare, and myalgia results from circulating cytokines.

CAN YOU EXPLAIN IT?

Explain why RSV can be severe in infants without the virus entering the bloodstream.

Model answer. RSV infects the respiratory tract and does not cause viremia. Severe disease comes from local infection in small airways, and the immune response contributes.
