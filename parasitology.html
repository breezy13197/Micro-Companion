Module 1 · Section 1

Parasite classification

Every parasite in this lecture is a eukaryote that lives at the host’s expense. The first sorting question is simple: one cell or many? The second depends on the answer: protozoa are sorted by where they live, and worms by what kind of worm they are.

Key points

  • Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotes. Helminths (worms) are multicellular metazoa.
  • Helminths fall into two phyla: Platyhelminthes (flatworms) and Nemathelminths (roundworms).
  • Protozoa are grouped by site of infection: intestinal (Entamoeba, Giardia, Cryptosporidium), urogenital (Trichomonas), and blood/tissue (Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, Trypanosoma, Leishmania).
  • Pneumocystis jirovecii is taught with the blood/tissue protozoa, but it is a fungus that was once classified as a protozoan.

Module 1 · Section 2

Form determines function

A protozoan changes form as it moves through its life cycle. Each form has a job: one feeds and divides, one survives the journey to the next host. Knowing which form is which tells you how the parasite spreads and what the lab will see.

Key points

  • Trophozoite: motile, metabolically active, feeding, and reproducing, with a flexible membrane. This form causes disease.
  • Cyst: nonmotile, nonmetabolizing, nonreproducing, with a thick wall. It survives in the environment and transmits infection.
  • Oocyst: the transmission form of Cryptosporidium (a cyst containing a zygote).
  • Promastigote and trypomastigote are flagellated forms (Leishmania, Trypanosoma); the amastigote has no flagellum.

Module 1 · Section 3

Hosts and transmission: break the chain

Every parasite needs a path from one host to the next. Name each link in that path, and you can see where a public health measure or a personal habit cuts it.

Key points

  • Definitive host: the sexual cycle occurs or the adult is present. Intermediate host: the asexual cycle occurs or larvae are present. Dead-end host: the larval form is not passed on.
  • Intestinal protozoa are swallowed as cysts (oocysts for Cryptosporidium).
  • Blood/tissue protozoa vary: insect vectors (Plasmodium, Trypanosoma, Leishmania), ingestion (Toxoplasma), inhalation (Pneumocystis).
  • Management interrupts the chain: sewage disposal, water purification, insect control, repellent, and bed nets.

Module 2 · Section 1

Entamoeba histolytica: from intestine to liver

Entamoeba is the invader among the intestinal protozoa. It starts as a swallowed cyst, becomes a trophozoite that eats into the colon wall, and can ride the portal blood to the liver. Each step explains a symptom, a lab finding, and a drug choice.

Key points

  • Cysts (four nuclei) are swallowed in fecally contaminated food or water. There is no animal reservoir.
  • Cysts differentiate in the ileum but colonize the cecum and colon. Trophozoites secrete enzymes that cause localized necrosis with little inflammation, forming a flask-shaped ulcer and bloody, mucous diarrhea.
  • Trophozoites can enter portal circulation and form a liver abscess: right upper-quadrant pain, fever, weight loss, and a tender, enlarged liver.
  • Treat tissue disease with metronidazole or tinidazole, then a luminal agent (paromomycin or iodoquinol) to clear cysts. Abscesses do not require drainage. Chlorination does not kill cysts; boiling and filtration do.

Module 2 · Section 2

Giardia and Cryptosporidium: the intestinal surface

Giardia and Cryptosporidium do not invade. They cling to the small intestine, and the trouble comes from what that does to absorption. With Cryptosporidium, the host’s immune status decides whether the illness lasts days or becomes debilitating.

Key points

  • Giardia: cysts excyst in the duodenum; trophozoites attach without invading. Inflammation causes malabsorption of fats and protein: watery, foul-smelling, nonbloody diarrhea, cramps, and flatulence for weeks to months, with no fever.
  • Giardia diagnosis: trophozoites or cysts in diarrheal stool, cysts in formed stool, the string test, and stool antigen (EIA, more sensitive than microscopy) or NAAT. Treat with tinidazole or metronidazole (alternatives: nitazoxanide, secnidazole).
  • Cryptosporidium: swallowed oocysts excyst in the small intestine and attach to the jejunal wall without invading. Self-limited in immunocompetent patients; severe, prolonged diarrhea with malnutrition and dehydration in immunocompromised patients.
  • Cryptosporidium diagnosis: oocysts on modified Kinyoun acid-fast stain, or stool antigen. Nitazoxanide if immunocompetent; ART reduces oocyst shedding in HIV. Cysts and oocysts resist chlorination; filter or boil water.

Module 2 · Section 3

Trichomonas vaginalis: interrupt reinfection

Trichomonas has no cyst and no life outside people. It passes directly between sexual partners as a trophozoite, which is why treating one person is often not enough.

Key points

  • Pear-shaped, with a central nucleus, four flagella, and an undulating membrane. Trophozoite only, no cyst form.
  • Sexually transmitted; the human genital tract is the reservoir. About 70–85% of infections cause few or no symptoms.
  • Women: watery, greenish, foul-smelling discharge with itching and burning. Men: about 10% have urethritis.
  • NAAT is the most sensitive test; a wet mount misses many cases. Treat with metronidazole (7 days in women; a single 2 g dose in men) or tinidazole, treat partners, and use condoms. There is no vaccine or prophylaxis.

Module 3 · Section 1

Build the malaria life cycle

Malaria moves through three places: the mosquito, the human liver, and human red blood cells. Each place hosts a different form of the parasite. Once you can place every form, the clinical course and every antimalarial drug in Module 4 have somewhere to land.

Key points

  • The female Anopheles mosquito is the definitive host and vector (sexual cycle). Humans are the intermediate host (asexual cycle).
  • Sporozoites (motile, spore-like) are injected and travel to the liver, where they become schizonts that divide by fission into merozoites.
  • Merozoites parasitize red blood cells, repeat the red cell cycle, and some become gametocytes that a mosquito takes up to restart the sexual cycle.
  • Hypnozoites of P. vivax and P. ovale stay dormant in the liver and can reactivate later. Symptoms typically begin about 2 weeks after the bite.

Module 3 · Section 3

Red cell destruction and severe malaria

Most of what a malaria patient feels comes from losing red blood cells. Cells burst when merozoites leave, and the spleen removes parasitized cells. P. falciparum adds a third problem: infected cells stick in small vessels.

Key points

  • Red cells are destroyed in two ways: merozoite release, and splenic trapping and lysis, which causes splenomegaly.
  • P. falciparum infects a greater percentage of red cells, of all ages, giving high parasitemia.
  • Its parasitized cells occlude capillaries, causing hemorrhage and necrosis, including in the brain (cerebral malaria). Extensive hemolysis leads to hemoglobinuria and acute renal failure.
  • Sickle cell heterozygotes are protected; this is a genetic, not an immune, defense.

Module 3 · Section 4

Virtual blood smear lab

Malaria is diagnosed on a blood film. Each test answers a different question: is a parasite there, which species is it, and how much of the blood is infected?

Key points

  • Blood films read by microscopy are the reference test. The thick smear detects parasites; the thin smear identifies the species and the percent of red cells infected.
  • One negative result does not rule malaria out: repeat every 12 to 24 hours for three sets.
  • Rapid diagnostic tests detect parasite antigens but cannot identify every species or measure parasitemia, so follow with microscopy.
  • PCR confirms the species but is usually too slow for acute diagnosis.

Module 4 · Section 2

Inside the parasite

Inside a red cell, the parasite digests hemoglobin in an acidic food vacuole and must neutralize the toxic heme that this releases. It also depends on its own mitochondrion and on folate for DNA. Each antimalarial class attacks one of these needs.

Key points

  • Heme detoxification: chloroquine (a weak base trapped in the acidic food vacuole), quinine, and lumefantrine block hemozoin formation, so toxic heme accumulates.
  • Endoperoxide activation: artemether and artesunate (prodrugs of dihydroartemisinin) carry an O–O bridge that heme iron activates, generating free radicals that damage parasite heme and proteins.
  • Mitochondria: atovaquone inhibits cytochrome bc1, blocking electron transport and pyrimidine synthesis.
  • Folate: proguanil’s metabolite cycloguanil inhibits DHFR (thymidine synthesis) and helps atovaquone collapse the mitochondrial membrane potential.

Module 4 · Section 3

Hypnozoites, G6PD, and relapse prevention

Primaquine and tafenoquine are the only drugs in this lecture that reach hypnozoites, and they carry a specific risk. They create oxidative stress, and a red cell that cannot make enough NADPH cannot defend itself.

Key points

  • G6PD is a red cell enzyme that generates NADPH, which regenerates glutathione, a powerful antioxidant.
  • Primaquine may generate reactive oxygen species. Avoid it in G6PD deficiency. Tafenoquine requires a quantitative G6PD test first.
  • Primaquine is given for 14 days; tafenoquine is a single dose (Krintafel with chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine, ages 16 and older; Arakoda for prophylaxis). Neither is used in pregnancy.
  • G6PD deficiency is also protective against malaria. Vertical integration: this is the pentose phosphate pathway and glutathione from your biochemistry course.

Module 5 · Section 1

Toxoplasma gondii: trace the transmission

The same parasite is a non-event for most healthy adults, a threat to a fetus, and a life-threatening brain infection in someone without T-cell immunity. What changes is the host.

Key points

  • Transmission: oocysts shed in cat feces, tissue cysts in undercooked meat, and mother to fetus during pregnancy.
  • Ingested cysts rupture in the small intestine; macrophages engulf the parasites, which become rapidly dividing tachyzoites. Cell-mediated immunity usually limits tachyzoite spread; parasites enter brain, muscle, and other tissues.
  • Immunocompetent adults: most asymptomatic, some mononucleosis-like. Immunocompromised: disseminated disease (encephalitis). Congenital: miscarriage, stillbirth, encephalitis, chorioretinitis, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, intracranial calcifications; most newborns look well but may develop chorioretinitis, intellectual disability, or blindness later.
  • Diagnosis: serology and PCR. Treatment: sulfadiazine plus pyrimethamine with leucovorin; TMP-SMX alternative; spiramycin in pregnancy, switching to sulfadiazine plus pyrimethamine with leucovorin if the fetus is infected. Prevention: cook meat thoroughly; pregnant women avoid cat litter.

Module 5 · Section 2

Pneumocystis jirovecii: the alveolus under siege

Pneumocystis is a fungus that was once classed as a protozoan. It is inhaled, and in a host with intact defenses it stays quiet. When defenses fall, it fills the alveoli with a frothy exudate that oxygen struggles to cross.

Key points

  • Inhaled; cysts in the alveoli induce an inflammatory response with plasma cells and a frothy exudate that disrupts oxygen exchange.
  • Pneumonia arises when host defenses are diminished. Its major surface glycoprotein varies (many genes, one expressed at a time).
  • Sudden fever, nonproductive cough, dyspnea, tachypnea. Untreated pneumonia mortality approaches 100%.
  • Treatment: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; add prednisone if PaO2 is below 70 mm Hg or the A-a gradient is 35 mm Hg or higher. Alternatives: pentamidine, atovaquone. Prophylaxis: TMP-SMX or aerosolized pentamidine when CD4 is below 200 cells/µL; also considered in transplant, hematologic cancer, and long-term glucocorticoid or other immunosuppressed patients.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

The best Pneumocystis pneumonia is the one prevented. Every patient with a CD4 count below 200 cells/µL, and many patients on long-term steroids or transplant drugs, should be screened for prophylaxis.
