Module 1 · Section 1

Staph or strep, at a glance

Every gram-positive coccus in this lecture eventually sorts into one of three genera: Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, or Enterococcus. Before any biochemical test, two things are visible on the bench and under the microscope — and they're often your fastest clue.

Key points

  • Arrangement: Staphylococcus grows in grape-like clusters; Streptococcus (and Enterococcus) grow in chains or pairs.
  • Catalase: bubbles on hydrogen peroxide mean Staphylococcus; no bubbles means Streptococcus or Enterococcus.
  • Both genera are facultative anaerobes, so oxygen tolerance alone will not separate them — you need catalase.

Module 1 · Section 2

Sorting the staphylococci

Once catalase tells you a coccus is a Staphylococcus, two more bench tests take you the rest of the way to a species: coagulase, and — for the coagulase-negative pair — novobiocin.

Key points

  • Coagulase splits S. aureus (positive; clots plasma) from the coagulase-negative staphylococci.
  • Novobiocin splits the coagulase-negative pair: S. epidermidis is susceptible (device infections); S. saprophyticus is resistant (UTI in young women).
  • Only S. aureus produces exotoxins among the three; S. epidermidis and S. saprophyticus do not.

Module 1 · Section 3

Sorting the streptococci and enterococcus

Streptococcus and Enterococcus share the catalase-negative, chain-forming pattern from Section 1. Hemolysis on blood agar is the next branch point, and one confirmatory test per branch finishes the job.

Key points

  • Beta-hemolysis (complete clearing) splits into bacitracin-sensitive group A strep and bacitracin-resistant, CAMP-positive group B strep.
  • Alpha-hemolysis (partial, green) splits into optochin-sensitive S. pneumoniae and optochin-resistant viridans streptococci.
  • Gamma-hemolysis (no clearing) and a colon origin point toward Enterococcus.

Module 2 · Section 1

S. aureus: from factor to disease

S. aureus causes more different diseases than any other organism in this lecture, and almost every one traces back to a single named toxin or enzyme. Learn the factor, and the disease follows.

Key points

  • Protein A blocks complement activation by binding IgG, reducing opsonization.
  • Three toxins drive three distinct diseases: TSST-1 (toxic shock, a superantigen), enterotoxin (fast-onset food poisoning, pre-formed in food), and exfoliatin (scalded skin syndrome, a protease).
  • Coagulase clots plasma to wall off infection; it is also the test that identifies S. aureus (Module 1).

Module 2 · Section 2

Group A strep: a case that unfolds

A single group A strep infection can cause three different kinds of trouble on three different timelines: pyogenic spread while the bacteria are actively growing, toxigenic disease from a superantigen, and immune-mediated complications that show up weeks later, after the throat infection is long gone.

Key points

  • M protein is explicitly the single most important S. pyogenes virulence factor: it blocks phagocytosis by inactivating C3b.
  • Erythrogenic toxin and pyrogenic exotoxin A are superantigens; they cause the scarlet fever rash and toxic shock, respectively.
  • Rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis are immune-mediated, appearing weeks after the infection — antibiotics for the throat infection reliably prevent the first, not the second.

Module 2 · Section 3

The rest of the gram-positive cocci in disease

Pneumococcus, viridans streptococci, enterococcus, and group B strep each have one clinical picture worth knowing cold. These are the decisions a pharmacist actually makes once the organism is known.

Key points

  • The pneumococcal capsule is the major antiphagocytic factor; a missing spleen leaves patients unable to clear it efficiently.
  • Viridans streptococci are oral flora linked to subacute endocarditis; Enterococcus is colon flora linked to UTI and endocarditis, including vancomycin-resistant strains treated with linezolid or daptomycin.
  • Group B strep colonizes the vagina; a positive prenatal screen means antibiotics during labor, not at the time of the screen.

The Neisseria Challenge

Neisseria meningitidis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae look almost identical under the microscope — both are gram-negative diplococci — but everything that matters clinically is different: where they live in the body, how they spread, what they cause, and how you treat and prevent them. Module 3 · Section 1

Meningococcus or gonococcus?

Both organisms are gram-negative diplococci, so a Gram stain alone will not tell them apart. Everything else does: where they enter the body, what protects them from the immune system, and one simple sugar test.

Key points

  • Portal of entry: N. meningitidis is respiratory; N. gonorrhoeae is genital (or vertical, at birth).
  • Signature virulence factor: a polysaccharide capsule and Factor H binding protein for meningococcus; pili for gonococcus. Both share IgA protease and endotoxin.
  • Diagnosis: maltose fermentation separates them (meningococcus positive, gonococcus negative); gonococcus is classically seen inside neutrophils on a genital Gram stain.

Module 3 · Section 2

Meningococcus: treatment, prophylaxis, and vaccines

A single meningococcal case triggers three separate decisions: what to give the patient, what to give the people around them, and what protects everyone else going forward.

Key points

  • Treatment: ceftriaxone or penicillin G.
  • Prophylaxis for close contacts: rifampin, ceftriaxone, or ciprofloxacin — substitute azithromycin for ciprofloxacin where resistant strains have appeared.
  • Vaccines: MenACWY (age 11-12, booster at 16) and MenB (shared decision, ideally 16-18); a combined MenABCWY product also exists.

Module 3 · Section 3

Gonococcus: treatment and prevention

Gonorrhea treatment comes down to a short list of if-then decisions: what's the allergy status, has chlamydia been ruled out, and when does the patient need to come back?

Key points

  • Standard treatment: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM, single dose. Add doxycycline only if chlamydia has not been excluded.
  • Cephalosporin allergy: gentamicin plus azithromycin.
  • Prevention: no vaccine exists — condoms and treating symptomatic patients and partners are the mainstay; erythromycin ointment prevents newborn conjunctivitis.

Module 4

Gram-Positive Rods & Toxin Syndromes

This module's organisms cause disease through four very different mechanisms: a three-part exotoxin that gets in through the skin or lungs (anthrax), neurotoxins that hijack the nervous system in opposite directions (botulism and tetanus), spores that exploit a course of antibiotics (C. difficile), and toxins that shut down protein synthesis or help a cell hide inside another cell (diphtheria and Listeria). Sorting infections by mechanism, not just by organism name, is what makes the rest of this module's facts stick.

  • Tell botulism, tetanus, and gas gangrene apart by toxin mechanism, clinical presentation, and treatment.
  • Trace C. difficile infection from antibiotic exposure to treatment and prevention, and connect Listeria and diphtheria to their signature virulence factors.
In this module
Module 4 · Section 1

Anthrax: which door did it come through?

Bacillus anthracis causes the same disease however it enters, but which door the spores came through changes everything about how it presents and how urgently it needs treatment. (Its cousin B. cereus causes a milder story: food poisoning from spores that germinate in improperly reheated food, such as rice, via two different enterotoxins.)

Key points

  • The exotoxin has three parts: protective antigen (binds host cells and delivers the other two), edema factor (an adenylate cyclase that raises cAMP), and lethal factor (a protease that causes cell death).
  • Cutaneous anthrax (spores enter a wound) is nearly all cases and produces a black eschar. Pulmonary anthrax (spores inhaled) is rare but far more dangerous.
  • The vaccine's immunogen is protective antigen itself.

Module 4 · Section 2

The toxin mechanism challenge

Botulism and tetanus are a classic look-alike pair: both toxins cut proteins that release neurotransmitter, but they act in opposite directions. Gas gangrene works by an entirely different mechanism — destroying membranes directly rather than touching the nervous system at all.

Key points

  • Botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release → descending, flaccid paralysis. Treated with antitoxin and respiratory support.
  • Tetanus toxin blocks glycine and GABA release in the spinal cord → spastic paralysis, lockjaw, opisthotonos. Metronidazole is often preferred over penicillin, which may antagonize GABA.
  • C. perfringens alpha toxin (lecithinase) destroys cell membranes directly → gas gangrene, tissue destruction. Treated with penicillin G plus debridement.

Module 4 · Section 3

C. difficile: the antibiotic-associated cycle

C. difficile is unusual in this lecture: the antibiotic itself sets up the infection. Everything about diagnosis, treatment, and prevention follows from that one fact.

Key points

  • Test only patients with new, unexplained diarrhea — colonization without symptoms is common.
  • Treatment: fidaxomicin preferred (initial and recurrent episodes); oral vancomycin acceptable; metronidazole reserved. Stop the causative antibiotic if possible.
  • Spores resist alcohol hand rub — soap and water is required, along with contact precautions and a sporicidal cleaner.

Module 4 · Section 4

Listeria and diphtheria: two more invaders

Two organisms, two very different ways of taking over a cell: Listeria breaks out of the compartment meant to destroy it, while diphtheria toxin shuts down the host cell's protein-making machinery entirely.

Key points

  • Listeria: internalin attaches, listeriolysin escapes the phagosome. Causes meningitis and sepsis in immunosuppressed adults, newborns, and pregnant women. Treated with ampicillin ± gentamicin (or TMP-SMX).
  • C. diphtheriae: club-shaped, "Chinese-letter" arrangement, no spores. Toxin ADP-ribosylates EF-2, halting protein synthesis — the same target as Pseudomonas exotoxin A.
  • Diphtheria diagnosis: tellurite agar culture plus the Elek test. Treatment: antitoxin plus penicillin or erythromycin; prevented by the toxoid vaccine (DTaP/Tdap/Td).

In this module
Module 5 · Section 1

E. coli: same organism, opposite diarrheas

Most E. coli is harmless gut flora. Two pathogenic strains tell almost opposite stories: one causes watery diarrhea through a toxin, the other causes bloody diarrhea through a different toxin entirely — with a treatment trap that makes the difference worth knowing cold.

Key points

  • ETEC: LT toxin raises cAMP, ST toxin raises cGMP — both act on the small intestine, causing watery diarrhea. Rehydration is the mainstay.
  • EHEC (O157:H7): Shiga toxin inhibits protein synthesis, causing bloody diarrhea and risking hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS).
  • In EHEC, avoid antimotility drugs, and use antibiotics cautiously — both may precipitate HUS.

Module 5 · Section 2

Salmonella, Shigella, and Klebsiella

Three gram-negative rods with three very different signatures — one survives inside immune cells, one spreads with almost nothing, and one is defined by a capsule so large it changes the look of the sputum.

Key points

  • Salmonella: non-lactose fermenter, H2S positive, survives inside macrophages. Non-typhoidal disease from poultry/eggs; S. Typhi causes typhoid fever.
  • Shigella: tiny infectious dose (10-200 organisms); S. dysenteriae makes Shiga toxin → bloody, mucoid dysentery.
  • Klebsiella: large capsule, mucoid colonies, currant-jelly sputum in pneumonia (classically with alcohol use disorder); resistance (ESBL, carbapenem-resistant) drives therapy.

Module 5 · Section 3

Vibrio, Campylobacter, and H. pylori

Three curved gram-negative rods, three distinct stories: a toxin mechanism worth knowing cold, a complication that shows up weeks later, and an organism that survives in an environment nothing else in this course does — the stomach.

Key points

  • Vibrio cholerae: cholera toxin locks Gs "on," raising cAMP → profuse, rice-water diarrhea. Rehydration is life-saving.
  • Campylobacter jejuni: curved "gull-wing" rod from poultry/unpasteurized milk. Can trigger Guillain-Barr\u00e9 syndrome or reactive arthritis weeks later.
  • H. pylori: urease neutralizes stomach acid. Causes gastritis, peptic ulcer, gastric cancer, and MALT lymphoma; treated with bismuth quadruple therapy.

Module 5 · Section 4

Where the enteric bacteria strike

Every organism in this module sorts by two questions: watery or bloody, and where. Answer those first, and the organism (and the treatment trap) usually follows.

Key points

  • Watery diarrhea usually means a toxin acting on the small intestine (ETEC, cholera), without invading tissue.
  • Bloody diarrhea usually means invasion or Shiga toxin in the colon (Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, EHEC).
  • H. pylori is the outlier: it lives in the stomach itself.

Module 6 · Section 1

Pseudomonas: the opportunist

Pseudomonas rarely troubles a healthy person. It strikes when the host is already vulnerable — immunocompromised, on a ventilator, living with cystic fibrosis, or recovering from a burn — and it is notoriously hard to clear once it arrives.

Key points

  • Chronically infects most adults with cystic fibrosis; contaminates IV fluids and respiratory equipment; a major cause of hospital-acquired infection.
  • Exotoxin A ADP-ribosylates EF-2 — the same mechanism as diphtheria toxin. Pyocyanin (the blue-green pigment) damages respiratory cilia.
  • Often resistant to many antibiotics; combination therapy or newer reserved agents (ceftazidime-avibactam and similar) are needed for difficult strains.

Module 6 · Section 2

H. influenzae and B. pertussis

Two respiratory organisms, both dramatically reduced by routine childhood vaccination — and both still worth knowing cold, because unvaccinated and undervaccinated patients still get sick.

Key points

  • H. influenzae is one of three encapsulated pyogens (with pneumococcus and meningococcus); type b is most antigenic. Treated with ceftriaxone.
  • B. pertussis produces pertussis toxin (ADP-ribosylates Gi → lymphocytosis), adenylate cyclase toxin, and tracheal cytotoxin. Diagnosed by PCR; treated with a macrolide.
  • Tdap is given in every pregnancy, to transfer protective antibodies to a newborn too young to be vaccinated directly.

Module 6 · Section 3

Tularemia and plague: zoonotic hazards

Both organisms come from animals, and both are laboratory hazards \u2014 the lab needs to know before it processes a specimen you suspect contains either one.

Key points

  • Francisella tularensis (tularemia): reservoir is rabbits, deer, and ticks. Endotoxin only, no exotoxin. Treated with a fluoroquinolone or doxycycline.
  • Yersinia pestis (plague): reservoir is rodents. Antiphagocytic capsule, endotoxin, exotoxin, and Yops. Treated with gentamicin or a fluoroquinolone.
  • Both are laboratory hazards, like anthrax — notify the lab whenever you suspect either one.

Module 6 · Section 4

Anaerobes and the big picture

This closing section does two things: covers the anaerobes that live wherever oxygen doesn't reach, and ties the whole course together around one question — which organisms have a capsule, and what happens with the one classic meningitis cause that doesn't.

Key points

  • Anaerobic abscesses: foul-smelling discharge, gas, necrotic tissue. Mouth anaerobes → aspiration/lung/brain abscess; colon anaerobes (B. fragilis) → intra-abdominal abscess.
  • Aminoglycosides fail against anaerobes because their uptake is oxygen-dependent.
  • The three classic encapsulated pyogens (pneumococcus, meningococcus, H. influenzae) resist phagocytosis via their capsule. Listeria is the exception — not encapsulated, escapes the phagosome via listeriolysin, and needs ampicillin, added specifically in newborns and in adults over 50 or immunocompromised.
