Module 1 · Section 1

Why antivirals are difficult

There are far fewer antiviral drugs than antibiotics, and the reason is not lack of effort. A virus has no machinery of its own for making proteins. It borrows the host cell’s, so much of what a virus does is also done by the patient’s own cells.

Key points

  • Selective toxicity means a drug harms the virus and spares the patient. Because viruses replicate with host machinery, this is harder than it is for bacteria.
  • Good targets are viral enzymes and processes that human cells lack, such as reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease, and neuraminidase.
  • Poor targets are shared host components, such as ribosomes and the host’s own DNA polymerases.
  • Three further obstacles: replication occurs while the patient feels well, some viruses are latent, and drug-resistant mutants can emerge.

Module 1 · Section 2

The replication roadmap

Every antiviral works at one stage of the viral replication cycle. Learn the eight stages once, and each new drug becomes a simple question: where does it sit on the map?

Key points

  • The eight stages are attachment and entry, uncoating, genome copying, integration, mRNA synthesis, protein making and cutting, assembly and packaging, and release.
  • The lecture groups drugs by the stage they block: early events, nucleic acid synthesis, integration, cleavage of precursor DNA, cleavage of precursor polypeptides, protein synthesis, release, and other mechanisms.
  • Integration belongs to retroviruses such as HIV. Other viruses skip that stage.
  • This roadmap is the frame for every later module: each drug class attaches to one stage.

Module 1 · Section 3

Target and consequence

Blocking one stage does not freeze the whole virus at once. It starves the stages that depend on it, while the stages before it still run. Knowing what keeps running explains how each class of drug works.

Key points

  • A block stalls the stages after it. The stages before it still run.
  • Blocking release lets new virions assemble, but they stay attached to the cell and cannot spread.
  • Blocking protein cutting leaves viral mRNA and long polypeptides in place, but no working proteins.
  • Which stages exist depends on the virus: integration is part of the HIV cycle and absent from influenza.

Module 2 · Section 1

Stop the virus at the door

A virus that never gets in cannot replicate. HIV entry runs through distinct steps, and different drugs act on different steps. Telling those steps apart is the whole skill here.

Key points

  • HIV entry has three steps: gp120 binds CD4 (attachment), gp120 then binds a coreceptor such as CCR5, and gp41 drives fusion of the viral envelope with the host membrane.
  • Maraviroc blocks CCR5 binding after CD4 attachment. Enfuvirtide is a synthetic peptide that binds gp41 and prevents fusion.
  • Ibalizumab is a monoclonal antibody against the host CD4 protein. It does not stop gp120 from attaching to CD4, but it blocks the steps after attachment, so HIV cannot engage the CCR5 and CXCR4 coreceptors.
  • Amantadine blocks the M2 channel of influenza A and prevents uncoating. It is no longer used to treat influenza.
  • Nirsevimab is a long-acting monoclonal antibody that prevents severe RSV disease in infants. It is passive immunization, not treatment.

Module 2 · Section 2

Build a chain terminator

Many antivirals imitate the building blocks a viral polymerase uses. The polymerase picks one up and builds it into the growing chain, and then a missing attachment point ends the chain.

Key points

  • A nucleoside is a nitrogenous base plus a sugar. Nucleoside analogs mimic that shape, so the polymerase builds them into the growing chain.
  • Many nucleoside analogs cause chain termination: they lack the 3′ hydroxyl needed to attach the next nucleotide.
  • Zidovudine has an azido group (N₃) in place of the 3′ hydroxyl.
  • Acyclovir is selective for two reasons: viral thymidine kinase phosphorylates it more effectively than the cellular enzyme, and viral DNA polymerase is inhibited more potently than the cellular polymerase.

Module 2 · Section 3

Polymerase challenge

Different viruses copy their genomes with different enzymes, and a drug has to match the enzyme. Learn the virus, the enzyme, and the drug class together, and a long list of names becomes a short set of patterns.

Key points

  • Some viruses copy their genome with their own DNA or RNA polymerase. Others use reverse transcriptase to copy RNA into DNA.
  • Herpesviruses: DNA polymerase, inhibited by acyclovir and ganciclovir (nucleoside analogs) and foscarnet (a pyrophosphate analog, not a nucleoside).
  • HIV: reverse transcriptase, inhibited by zidovudine and lamivudine (nucleoside analogs) and efavirenz (a nonnucleoside inhibitor).
  • Hepatitis B: reverse transcriptase, inhibited by entecavir and tenofovir. Hepatitis C: NS5B RNA polymerase, inhibited by sofosbuvir, and the NS5A protein, inhibited by velpatasvir. Ribavirin acts on nucleic acid synthesis in other viruses.
  • Foscarnet fits neither class. It blocks the pyrophosphate site and needs no activation, unlike acyclovir.

Module 3 · Section 1

Integration

Integrase is a viral enzyme with no human counterpart, which makes it a clean target. HIV also mutates quickly, so the drugs are used together. This section covers both ideas.

Key points

  • Integrase is an enzyme produced by retroviruses. It inserts the viral genome into the DNA of the host cell.
  • Raltegravir and dolutegravir are integrase inhibitors, used primarily as treatment against HIV.
  • Blocking integrase leaves reverse transcription running, but the viral DNA is never inserted into the host genome, so new viral mRNA is not made.
  • HIV is treated with a combination of drugs, not one drug alone. Most recommended starting regimens pair an integrase inhibitor with one or two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, for example dolutegravir plus lamivudine.
  • Why combine: a resistant mutant must overcome several drugs at once, so resistance emerges more slowly. Resistance testing guides the choice, and dolutegravir has a high barrier to resistance.

Module 3 · Section 2

Capsids and packaging

Late in the cycle, a virus builds capsids, fills them with genome, and releases virions. Two drug classes act here: one on the capsid itself, and one on the enzyme that prepares DNA for packaging. In the lecture’s examples, both are used for prevention.

Key points

  • The capsid is the protein shell around the viral genome.
  • Lenacapavir (Yeztugo) is a capsid inhibitor. It binds the capsid protein and disrupts several steps: nuclear entry of viral DNA, virus assembly and release, and formation of the capsid core (capsids come out malformed).
  • Lenacapavir was approved in June 2025 as a twice-yearly injection for HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP): prevention, not treatment.
  • Terminase is a viral enzyme that cleaves concatenated viral DNA in preparation for virion assembly. Letermovir inhibits terminase.
  • Letermovir prevents cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection and disease in high-risk transplant recipients: prophylaxis, not treatment. Ganciclovir is a CMV treatment drug.

Module 3 · Section 3

Cut to function

Some viruses make their proteins as one long polypeptide and rely on a viral protease to cut it into working pieces. A drug that fits the protease blocks the cutting. Ritonavir has a second job as well: it raises the levels of other drugs.

Key points

  • In some viruses, the mRNA is translated into a long polypeptide that contains multiple enzymes. Virus-specific proteases cut it into functional proteins. This applies to HIV, hepatitis C virus, and SARS-CoV-2.
  • Nucleoside analogs mimic a nucleoside. Peptidomimetics mimic a stretch of peptide: the drug fits the protease active site and blocks cleavage. Example: nirmatrelvir (SARS-CoV-2 main protease). Shape decides action.
  • Examples: HIV, atazanavir and ritonavir (booster), used as treatment and always in combination with other drugs. Hepatitis C, glecaprevir (with pibrentasvir). SARS-CoV-2, nirmatrelvir (with ritonavir).
  • Ritonavir inhibits CYP3A, the liver enzyme that breaks down many drugs. In Paxlovid it slows the metabolism of nirmatrelvir so its levels stay high. It also boosts other HIV protease inhibitors, such as darunavir.
  • The same effect causes drug interactions. Paxlovid carries a boxed warning for significant drug interactions, so review every medicine the patient takes before dispensing.

Module 4 · Section 1

Stop release

Influenza virions leave an infected cell only if an enzyme cuts them loose. Block that enzyme and the virions are built but trapped. This is the last stage of the roadmap, so everything before it still runs.

Key points

  • Hemagglutinin on the virion binds sialic acid on the host cell. Neuraminidase cleaves it, so new virions can be released.
  • Oseltamivir inhibits influenza neuraminidase, which prevents viral release. It is effective against influenza A and B, and against strains that are amantadine resistant.
  • Zanamivir and peramivir are other neuraminidase inhibitors.
  • Oseltamivir is a treatment for influenza. Start it as soon as possible if the patient is hospitalized, and within 2 days of symptoms if the patient is an outpatient.

Module 4 · Section 2

Steal the cap

Influenza starts its mRNA with a cap taken from host mRNA, and that cap helps induce translation of the viral mRNA. Baloxavir blocks the enzyme that cuts the cap off. This section also covers interferon, another drug that works at a different stage.

Key points

  • Cap-snatching: viral mRNA “snatches” the host 5′ cap to induce translation of the viral mRNA.
  • Baloxavir is the first-in-class cap-snatching inhibitor. It inhibits the endonuclease enzyme responsible for cleaving host mRNA.
  • Baloxavir treats influenza A and B. It is also used for post-exposure prophylaxis: a single dose within 48 hours of contact (ages 5 and older).
  • Interferon: recombinant interferon alpha inhibits viral protein synthesis. It is used for chronic hepatitis B, and historically for hepatitis C (now replaced by direct-acting antivirals).

Module 4 · Section 3

Treat or prevent?

The same stage of replication can be attacked for different clinical goals. For every patient, ask in order: is the goal treatment or prevention, which drug fits, and what must be checked before it is dispensed?

Key points

  • Treatment is for established infection. Prevention (prophylaxis) is given before disease develops.
  • Letermovir: prevention, for high-risk transplant recipients. Oseltamivir: treatment of influenza. Nirmatrelvir with ritonavir: treatment of mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in high-risk adults. Ensitrelvir: post-exposure prevention of COVID-19, ages 12 and older. Nirsevimab: prevention by passive immunization in infants.
  • Baloxavir does both: it treats influenza A and B and is used for post-exposure prophylaxis.
  • Before dispensing nirmatrelvir with ritonavir, review every medicine the patient takes. Paxlovid carries a boxed warning for significant drug interactions.

Module 5 · Section 1

Live, killed, and subunit

Every vaccine teaches the immune system to recognize a virus without the full disease. The method decides how strong the response is, how long it lasts, and who can safely receive it.

Key points

  • Vaccines are used to induce active immunity.
  • Killed (inactivated) vaccines contain killed virus. Live-attenuated vaccines contain a weakened virus that rarely causes disease in healthy people, but it can revert or cause disease in immunocompromised patients. Subunit vaccines contain purified proteins.
  • Live vaccines give longer, stronger protection and interrupt transmission more effectively. Live vaccines are generally preferred, but they carry risks.
  • Secretory IgA prevents viral attachment to mucous membranes. Live vaccines given by the natural route, for example the nasal influenza vaccine, produce it.
  • Killed and subunit vaccines contain no live virus, so they cannot revert to virulence, spread to contacts, or cause disease.

Module 5 · Section 2

Build a vaccine platform

Three newer platforms appear throughout practice: mRNA, recombinant subunit, and viral vector. They differ in what the patient receives and in who makes the antigen.

Key points

  • mRNA: mRNA instructions are delivered to cells, which make the viral protein (antigen). Examples: COVID-19 (Comirnaty, Spikevax) and RSV (mRESVIA).
  • Recombinant subunit: a purified or recombinant viral protein. Some products add an adjuvant to boost the response. Examples: Shingrix (zoster), Heplisav-B (hepatitis B), and Arexvy and Abrysvo (RSV).
  • Viral vector: a harmless virus carries the gene for a viral protein into cells. Example: Ervebo (Ebola), a live, attenuated recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus.
  • In mRNA and viral vector vaccines, the patient’s own cells make the antigen. In a recombinant subunit vaccine, the protein is delivered ready-made.

Module 5 · Section 3

Vaccine safety challenge

Choosing a vaccine means matching the platform’s properties to the patient. For an immunocompromised patient, whether the vaccine contains a live, replicating virus is the property that matters most.

Key points

  • Live-attenuated vaccines can cause disease in immunocompromised patients. Killed and subunit vaccines contain no live virus and cannot.
  • Live vaccines can revert to virulence and spread to nonimmune contacts. Killed and subunit vaccines cannot.
  • Stability at room temperature: low for live, high for killed, and it varies for subunit vaccines.
  • Common live vaccines: measles, mumps, rubella, varicella, rotavirus. Killed: hepatitis A, rabies. Subunit: hepatitis B (recombinant), HPV, and zoster (Shingrix).

Module 6 · Section 1

Active vs. passive immunity

A patient can be protected in two very different ways: by building their own immune response, or by receiving antibodies that were made elsewhere. They differ in how fast they start, how long they last, and whether the body remembers.

Key points

  • Active immunity: the patient’s own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells, after infection or vaccination. Onset takes days to weeks. It is long-lasting because of immune memory. Examples: measles vaccine, Shingrix.
  • Passive immunity: pre-formed antibodies (immunoglobulins) are given to the patient. Onset is immediate. It is temporary, lasting weeks to months. Examples: rabies immune globulin, and nirsevimab for infants.
  • Passive protection leaves no immune memory, because the patient’s own immune system did not make the response.

Module 6 · Section 2

Immediate and durable protection

Sometimes a patient needs protection right now and also for the long term. Rabies after an exposure is the classic case. One product covers the gap, and the other builds lasting immunity.

Key points

  • Passive-active immunity: after a rabies exposure, immune globulin gives immediate protection while the first vaccine dose starts active immunity.
  • The vaccine alone would leave a gap, because active immunity takes days to weeks to start.
  • Immune globulin alone would not last, because passive antibodies fade and no immune memory forms.
  • Together, the passive antibodies cover the early period, and active immunity takes over as they fade.

Module 6 · Section 3

Herd immunity simulator

A vaccinated person is protected directly. When enough people in a community are immune, people who are not immune are protected indirectly too. How many is enough depends on the disease.

Key points

  • Measles: about 95% of people must be immune to stop sustained spread (WHO).
  • Picture 100 people, 95 of them immune. Each case infects fewer than one other person, so the outbreak fades.
  • The 5 who are not immune (infants, immunocompromised patients) are protected indirectly.
  • Polio: about 80% (WHO). The threshold depends on the disease.
  • If coverage falls below the threshold, outbreaks return.
