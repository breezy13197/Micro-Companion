Module 1 · Section 1

Five viruses, one organ

All five hepatitis viruses infect the liver and cause a similar illness. That similarity is why symptoms alone cannot name the virus, and it is why the rest of this module focuses on how the viruses differ.

Key points

  • Hepatitis A, B, C, D, and E all infect the liver, and each has surface proteins that react with receptors on hepatocytes.
  • The shared picture is fever, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, dark urine, pale feces, and elevated transaminase levels.
  • In HAV and HBV the virus is not directly cytotoxic. Cytotoxic T cells that target viral antigen on the hepatocyte surface cause the damage.
  • HDV is the exception: delta antigen is cytopathic in hepatocytes.

HAV and HBV are not directly cytotoxic: the T-cell response to viral antigen on the hepatocyte surface does the damage. Delta antigen is the exception.

Module 1 · Section 2

Telling A through E apart

Four features separate the five viruses: the family and genome, the route of spread, whether a chronic carrier state occurs, and whether a vaccine or immune globulin exists.

Key points

  • HAV and HEV spread by the fecal–oral route and do not cause a chronic carrier state in healthy people.
  • HBV, HCV, and HDV spread through blood (and sexual contact), and each can become chronic. HBV also passes from mother to child at birth.
  • Only HAV and HBV have vaccines. HEV has none in the U.S. (Hecolin is licensed in China).
  • HBV is the only DNA virus of the five. It carries a DNA polymerase that also works as a reverse transcriptase. HDV is a satellite that needs HBV.

Module 1 · Section 3

Age, exposure, and prevention

Prevention depends on the patient in front of you. The same virus calls for a different action depending on age, the time since exposure, and pregnancy.

Key points

  • HAV: incubation is 3 to 4 weeks, and most cases resolve in under 8 weeks. About 70% of children under 6 have no symptoms. There is no chronic state and no antiviral. After an exposure, give vaccine, plus immune globulin when indicated, within 2 weeks.
  • HBV: give the birth-dose vaccine within 24 hours, then 2 or 3 more doses. Newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers also receive HBIG. Chronic infection (more than 6 months) raises the risk of cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer.
  • HCV: CDC recommends one-time screening of every adult 18 and older, and of pregnant women in every pregnancy. More than half of acute infections become chronic.
  • HEV: mortality is below 0.1% in most people but reaches up to about 20–25% in third-trimester pregnancy. Chronic infection can occur in immunocompromised patients, and ribavirin has shown efficacy there.

Module 2 · Section 1

Reading HBV serology

HBV serology is a set of antigens and antibodies that rise and fall in a pattern. Read together, they tell you whether a patient is newly infected, chronically infected, or protected.

Key points

  • HBsAg (surface antigen) is present in acute and chronic infection. Chronic infection means it persists for more than 6 months.
  • Anti-HBs (surface antibody) shows recovery or vaccine immunity. IgM anti-HBc (core antibody) shows recent infection.
  • HBeAg and anti-HBe are the e antigen and its antibody. HBeAg is a marker of active replication.
  • Seroconversion means an antigen disappears and its antibody appears. Two separate events: HBeAg to anti-HBe, and HBsAg to anti-HBs.

A schematic, not patient data. In this pattern each antigen falls as its antibody appears: HBeAg gives way to anti-HBe, and HBsAg gives way to anti-HBs.

Module 2 · Section 2

HBV replication and cccDNA

HBV looks like a DNA virus, but it copies itself through an RNA intermediate. That one unusual step gives us our best drugs, and one unusual DNA form explains why we still cannot cure it.

Key points

  • HBV carries its own DNA polymerase that also works as a reverse transcriptase. It is one of the few DNA viruses to do so, and it is the target of the first-line drugs entecavir and tenofovir.
  • In chronic infection, covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) forms and stays in the hepatocyte nucleus. It is the template for the mRNA that makes viral proteins and new genomes.
  • cccDNA cannot be targeted or degraded by current therapies, so hepatitis B cannot be completely cured. The treatment goal is undetectable HBV DNA.
  • Acute infection: no antiviral. Chronic infection: entecavir or tenofovir, usually continued for life. Prevention: HBsAg subunit vaccine and HBIG.

Module 2 · Section 3

HCV: SVR and direct-acting antivirals

Hepatitis C is curable. Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) each block one protein of the virus, and combining them raises the chance of a cure.

Key points

  • Treatment goal: SVR, sustained virologic response, meaning undetectable HCV RNA 12 weeks after treatment is complete. Achieving SVR reduces liver cancer and end-stage liver disease.
  • NS5B polymerase inhibitor: sofosbuvir. NS5A inhibitors: velpatasvir and pibrentasvir (NS5A is a cofactor in the activity of the RNA polymerase). NS3/4A protease inhibitor: glecaprevir.
  • Combination therapy has a higher chance of SVR. Guideline regimens: sofosbuvir/velpatasvir for 12 weeks, or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir for 8 weeks.
  • HCV is an RNA virus. About 2.4 million people in the U.S. have chronic HCV, and more than half of acute infections become chronic. No vaccine is available.

Each direct-acting antiviral blocks one viral protein. Guideline regimens combine them, which raises the chance of SVR.

Module 2 · Section 4

HDV: a virus that borrows an envelope

Hepatitis D virus is incomplete on its own. It can only copy itself inside cells that already make HBV’s surface protein, which explains how it spreads, why it is dangerous, and how it is prevented.

Key points

  • HDV lacks the gene for an envelope protein, so it cannot replicate alone. It replicates in HBV-infected cells and uses HBsAg as its envelope. A virus that relies on another in this way is a satellite.
  • The genome is single-stranded, negative-sense, covalently closed circular RNA that encodes one protein: delta antigen, which is cytopathic in hepatocytes.
  • Coinfection: HBV and HDV are acquired together. Superinfection: HDV is acquired by someone who already has HBV. Superinfection in chronic HBV carriers carries a much higher risk of fulminant hepatitis, chronic hepatitis, and liver failure.
  • Treatment: peginterferon alpha may reduce the effects of chronic hepatitis but does not eradicate the carrier state. Bulevirtide blocks the NTCP receptor. Prevention: a person immunized against HBV will not acquire HDV.

HDV carries its own small RNA and delta antigen, but it has no gene for an envelope protein. It wraps itself in HBsAg made by a coinfecting HBV.

Arboviruses and vector-borne transmission

In this module
Module 3 · Section 1

Vectors and the transmission cycle

An arbovirus has to be transmitted by an arthropod, and it multiplies in both the arthropod and the vertebrate host. The cycle only works if the virus is present in enough quantity at each hand-off.

Key points

  • Arbovirus means any virus transmitted by arthropod vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks. More than 600 viruses are included, typically named for the disease they cause or where they were first isolated.
  • Transmission requires a sufficient amount of virus in the host bloodstream.
  • Only female mosquitoes transmit virus, because only females take blood from humans.
  • The extrinsic incubation period is the time needed for the mosquito’s saliva to contain enough virus to transmit an infectious dose.
  • Spread within the human body is systemic: the virus travels through the bloodstream (viremia).

Module 3 · Section 2

Dead-end hosts and reservoirs

Whether a human can pass an arbovirus back to a mosquito depends on how much virus is in the blood and for how long. That one variable decides whether the person is a dead end or a source.

Key points

  • Infection in the arthropod or the natural vertebrate host usually does not cause disease. Disease manifests in the dead-end host.
  • Dead-end host: the concentration of virus in the blood is too low, and viremia too brief, for transmission to another vector.
  • Occasionally high-level viremia occurs and humans become reservoirs, as with yellow fever and dengue.
  • Pathogenesis combines cytocidal effects of the virus and immunopathologic effects of the host response.

Module 3 · Section 3

Four arboviruses compared

The same four viruses appear again and again in cases. Each has a distinct vector, a distinct syndrome, and a distinct answer to “is there a vaccine?”

Key points

  • Most arbovirus infections are asymptomatic. Symptomatic infection is an acute febrile illness, and severity ranges from mild to fatal.
  • Consequences include encephalitis, hemorrhagic fever, and fever with myalgia, arthralgia, and a nonhemorrhagic rash.
  • Yellow fever has a single-dose vaccine with lifelong protection. A dengue vaccine (QDenga) is licensed in some countries. West Nile and Zika have no vaccine.

In this module
Module 4 · Section 1

Rabies: a virus that travels by nerve

Rabies is a nerve virus. It hides at the bite site, climbs the nerves into the brain, and then travels back out to the salivary glands, never relying on the bloodstream.

Key points

  • Reservoirs: bats, skunks, and raccoons in the U.S., and dogs worldwide (the most common reservoir).
  • The virus is enveloped with a bullet-shaped capsid and a negative-sense single-stranded RNA genome. There is a single serotype, and the envelope glycoprotein spikes provide antigenicity.
  • It replicates at the bite site, infects sensory neurons, and moves to the CNS, where it is immunologically shielded. Then it moves down peripheral nerves to the salivary glands and enters saliva.
  • Negri bodies are cytoplasmic inclusions in infected neurons. Furious rabies (agitation, delirium, seizures, hydrophobia) and paralytic rabies (about 20% of cases, ascending paralysis) are the two forms. Once symptoms begin, rabies is almost always fatal.

Antigenicity comes from the envelope glycoprotein spikes, and there is a single serotype.

Module 4 · Section 2

Rabies exposure management

Rabies cannot be treated once symptoms start, so the pharmacist’s job is the first hour after the exposure. WHO exposure categories tell you exactly how much to do.

Key points

  • First step: wash the wound with soap and water for at least 15 minutes.
  • Category I (touching or feeding animals, licks on intact skin): wash exposed skin; no PEP needed.
  • Category II (nibbling of uncovered skin, minor scratches or abrasions without bleeding): wound washing and immediate vaccination.
  • Category III (bites or scratches through the skin, saliva on mucous membranes or broken skin, direct contact with bats): wound washing, immediate vaccination, and rabies immune globulin or monoclonal antibodies.

Module 4 · Section 3

Ebola: structure and how it kills

Ebola virus is long, filamentous, and carries its own polymerase. It causes a systemic disease in which damage to vessels, a failing immune response, and liver failure combine.

Key points

  • Ebola virus is enveloped with a helical nucleocapsid and a non-segmented, negative-sense ssRNA genome. It carries an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.
  • Fruit bats are thought to be the natural hosts. Human-to-human spread is through blood and body fluids, and needle or syringe reuse is also implicated. Virus may persist in semen of survivors, documented up to 15 months after recovery (WHO).
  • The glycoprotein kills endothelial cells, causing severe thrombocytopenia and hemorrhage. Two other proteins inhibit interferon induction and action.
  • Lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells die, so the antibody response is ineffective. Hepatocytes die, causing liver failure. Case-fatality averages about 50% (range 25–90%).

Some Ebola virions curl at one end into a “shepherd’s crook” shape in electron micrographs.

Ebola species and countermeasures

Ebola is not one virus. There are six species, and the vaccine and antibodies you can use depend on which species the patient has.

Key points

  • Six species exist. Zaire, Sudan, and Bundibugyo cause large outbreaks.

  • No approved vaccine or antibody product exists for Sudan or Bundibugyo virus. Zaire-specific products cannot be assumed to work against them.
  • The lecture reports a 2026 Bundibugyo epidemic in the DRC with about 48% fatality (figures as of 27 September 2026). These numbers change weekly, so check WHO or CDC.

Module 5 · Section 1

The HIV virion and CD4 loss

HIV is a retrovirus, which means it copies its RNA genome into DNA and inserts it into the host. Its structure explains how it enters cells and why the immune system collapses.

Key points

  • HIV is a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus that carries two identical copies of its genome.
  • Its envelope contains gp120 and gp41, which bind the CD4 and CCR5 receptors on T cells.
  • It infects and kills helper CD4 T lymphocytes, causing loss of cell-mediated immunity and a higher risk of opportunistic infections. Other cells that display CD4 can also be infected.
  • Inside the virion are the enzymes reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease.

The HIV life cycle

The life cycle has seven steps. Each antiretroviral class blocks one of them, so the order is the map you will use for the rest of the module.

Key points

  • Binding (CD4, then co-receptor) → fusion → reverse transcription → integration → replication → assembly → budding.
  • Persistence: the integrated DNA copy stays in the host genome, so antiretroviral therapy suppresses HIV but does not cure it. This parallels HBV cccDNA.
  • hiv.gov: ART is not a cure, and the virus remains in the body even when the viral load is undetectable.

Module 5 · Section 3

Where each drug class acts

Each antiretroviral name belongs to a class, and each class blocks a specific step. Placing a drug on the life cycle tells you what it does.

Key points

  • Entry inhibitors: enfuvirtide is a biomimetic peptide that binds gp41 and blocks fusion. Maraviroc binds the host CCR5 co-receptor and blocks co-receptor binding.
  • Nucleoside analogs (emtricitabine, lamivudine, tenofovir) inhibit reverse transcriptase.
  • Integrase inhibitors (raltegravir, dolutegravir) block integration of the viral genome into the host genome.
  • Protease inhibitors (saquinavir, darunavir) leave non-infectious, immature virus particles.

Each class blocks one step. Entry inhibitors act on two of the first steps, and protease inhibitors act late, so particles are released immature.

Module 5 · Section 4

How NRTIs stop DNA synthesis

Nucleoside analogs look like the building blocks reverse transcriptase uses. Reverse transcriptase builds them in, and the strand cannot grow past that point.

Key points

  • Emtricitabine, lamivudine, and tenofovir are converted in the cell to the triphosphate form.
  • They compete with the matching natural nucleotide: dCTP for the cytosine analogs, dATP for tenofovir.
  • When an analog is added, the next nucleotide cannot be added, and chain elongation halts (chain termination).
  • Structure clue: none of the drawn structures has a 3′-OH group, which is the attachment point for the next nucleotide.

Module 5 · Section 5

Protease inhibitors and boosting

Protease inhibitors work on the viral protease dimer. Several are given with a second drug, a booster, that does not fight HIV at all, but changes how the body clears the first drug.

Key points

  • Protease monomers form a dimer, and the dimer cuts the viral polypeptide into working proteins.
  • Saquinavir is a peptide mimic that binds the active site of the dimerized protease. It does not bind the monomer.
  • Darunavir is given with a booster, ritonavir or cobicistat, that inhibits CYP3A. Darunavir levels rise, and so does the risk of drug interactions.
  • The 24 September 2026 DHHS update lists boosted darunavir plus lamivudine or emtricitabine as an option.

Saquinavir binds the dimerized form and does not bind the monomer.

Module 6 · Section 1

Combination therapy and resistance

HIV is treated with several drugs at once, and the drugs only work if the patient actually takes them. Both facts come from the same cause: the virus mutates, and incomplete treatment lets resistant virus win.

Key points

  • Missed doses or an incomplete regimen let resistant virus develop (hiv.gov).
  • Drugs from different classes block different steps, so one mutation is less likely to defeat the whole regimen.
  • A typical starting regimen is three medicines from at least two drug classes (hiv.gov).
  • The model in the activity is a teaching simplification with made-up numbers. It shows the direction of the effect, not real patient risk.

Module 6 · Section 3

Preventing HIV: PrEP, PEP, and more

There is no HIV vaccine, so prevention combines drugs taken before exposure, drugs taken after exposure, and behaviors and treatment that lower transmission.

Key points

  • PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis): daily oral tenofovir-based combinations such as tenofovir/emtricitabine, or long-acting injectables: cabotegravir every 2 months or lenacapavir every 6 months.
  • PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis): antiretrovirals started after a possible exposure.
  • Circumcision reduces infection. Use condoms and avoid sharing needles.
  • A sustained undetectable viral load prevents sexual transmission (hiv.gov).

Module 6 · Section 4

HIV in pregnancy: delivery and feeding

Pregnancy guidance turns on one number: the HIV RNA near delivery. This guidance changed recently, so older rules to avoid breastfeeding no longer apply as written.

Key points

  • Antiretroviral therapy is given to the mother and to the newborn.
  • Delivery (DHHS): scheduled cesarean at 38 weeks only when HIV RNA is above 1,000 copies/mL or unknown near delivery. At 1,000 copies/mL or below, vaginal birth follows the usual obstetric indications.
  • Infant feeding (DHHS, 2023): with a sustained undetectable viral load, breastfeeding is supported through shared decision-making (risk under 1%, but not zero). Formula or donor milk removes the postnatal risk.
