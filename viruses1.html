Module 1 · Section 1

Virus or cell?

Before you build a virus, decide what counts as one. A virus carries a genome and a protective coat, but it lacks most of what lets a cell live on its own.

Key points

  • A virus carries DNA or RNA, not both. A cell carries both.
  • Viruses borrow host ribosomes and enzymes, which makes them obligate intracellular parasites.
  • One infected cell can release hundreds of progeny viruses.
  • Some viruses wear a lipoprotein envelope. Every cell has a lipoprotein membrane.
  • Footnote from the lecture: arenaviruses package a few nonfunctional ribosomes.

Module 1 · Section 2

Build a virion

Every virion solves two problems: protect a small genome, and get it into the next cell. Each part you add solves one of them.

Key points

  • The capsid is built from repeating units called capsomers (or protomers).
  • Capsid plus genome is the nucleocapsid. Most capsids are icosahedral (20 sides, looks spherical) or helical (a hollow, rod-shaped coil).
  • An envelope is host-derived lipid plus virus-specific proteins. Matrix proteins link it to the capsid.
  • Surface proteins, on the capsid or in the envelope, mediate attachment and are the main antigens.

Module 1 · Section 3

Envelope consequences

The envelope is a thin lipid layer, yet it changes how a virus survives outside the body, how it is killed, and how it dodges antibodies.

Key points

  • Enveloped viruses are more sensitive to heat, drying, detergents, and solvents.
  • Nonenveloped viruses persist on surfaces much longer.
  • Envelope glycoproteins can vary, which helps some viruses evade the immune response.
  • Herpesviruses are enveloped. Noroviruses are not.

Replicate It

Every virus must get in, get copied, get built, and get out. Each of those steps is also a place where a drug can interfere. You will order the cycle, match drugs to the steps they block, and then follow a bacteriophage down two different paths.

In this module
Module 2 · Section 1

Put the cycle in order

The order is fixed, so a block at one step stops every step after it. That is why the cycle matters to anyone who dispenses antivirals.

Key points

  • Attachment, entry, and uncoating: the virion binds host receptors, enters, and releases its genome.
  • Genome replication: a viral polymerase copies the genome and makes mRNA.
  • Retroviruses add two steps: reverse transcriptase makes DNA from RNA, and integrase inserts it into host DNA.
  • Protein processing: host ribosomes make viral proteins, and a viral protease cuts long precursors.
  • Assembly and release: new virions assemble and leave by lysis or budding.

Module 2 · Section 2

Where would a drug work?

Antiviral drug classes are named for the step they block. Link each step to its target here; the detailed pharmacology comes in Viruses II.

Key points

  • Entry inhibitors block attachment and entry. Examples: maraviroc and enfuvirtide for HIV.
  • Polymerase inhibitors and nucleoside analogs block genome replication. Examples: acyclovir for HSV, sofosbuvir for HCV.
  • Reverse transcriptase and integrase inhibitors block the retrovirus steps. Examples: tenofovir and dolutegravir.
  • Protease inhibitors block protein processing. Examples: HIV protease inhibitors and nirmatrelvir for SARS-CoV-2.
  • Neuraminidase inhibitors act at assembly and release. Example: oseltamivir for influenza.

Module 2 · Section 3

Lytic or lysogenic?

A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria. After its DNA enters, it takes one of two paths, and the choice decides whether the bacterium survives.

Key points

  • Lytic cycle: many progeny are made, and the cell ruptures to release them. In some cases progeny leave without damaging the cell.
  • Lysogenic cycle: phage DNA is incorporated into the host genome, and progeny are not made until the phage is stimulated.
  • Integrated viral DNA is the prophage.
  • When phage DNA is excised, part of the bacterial DNA may leave with it: specialized transduction.

Module 2 · Section 4

When a phage changes a bacterium

A phage can change what a bacterium is capable of without changing its species. This is lysogenic conversion.

Key points

  • Integration of a phage can increase the virulence of a bacterium.
  • Corynebacterium diphtheriae produces diphtheria toxin after it has been infected by phage β.
  • Shiga toxin in E. coli (STEC) is carried by a prophage.
  • S. aureus prophages can carry virulence and immune-evasion genes.

Change It

Viruses do not stay the same. A single mutation can let a virus slip past antibodies or past a drug, and two viruses in one cell can swap genetic material. Here you make those changes yourself, then see how scientists turn viruses into delivery tools and sort them into families.

In this module
Module 3 · Section 1

Make a mutation

A mutation is just a change in the genome. Whether it matters depends on which protein it lands in, and which defense that protein meets.

Key points

  • A mutation can alter a surface antigen so existing antibodies no longer recognize it. The result is an antigenic variant.
  • Small, gradual changes in influenza surface proteins are antigenic drift.
  • A mutation in a viral drug target can create a drug-resistant mutant.
  • Error-prone polymerases, as in influenza viruses, HIV, and hepatitis C virus, are a source of mutations.

Module 3 · Section 2

Drift, shift, or swap?

Mutation changes a genome one letter at a time. When two different viruses infect the same cell, they can change it much faster, by exchanging whole pieces.

Key points

  • Recombination: exchange of genetic information between homologous regions of chromosomes. It occurs in DNA viruses and is also documented in RNA viruses, including coronaviruses.
  • Reassortment: exchange of genetic information within a segmented genome. It occurs at higher frequency than recombination, for example in influenza.
  • Reassortment produces new flu strains: antigenic shift.
  • Gradual change from mutation is antigenic drift.

Module 3 · Section 3

Viruses as gene-delivery tools

The same ability that makes viruses dangerous, entering cells efficiently, makes them useful. Gene therapy borrows it to deliver a working gene.

Key points

  • Vectors are modified so they cannot cause disease.
  • AAV: not known to cause disease in humans, low immunogenicity, mostly stays outside host chromosomes. Examples: Zolgensma, Luxturna, Hemgenix.
  • Lentivirus (a retrovirus): integrates into host DNA for long-lasting expression, with a small risk of insertional mutagenesis. Often used to modify cells outside the body.
  • Adenovirus: carries large genes, but a strong immune response limits repeat dosing.
  • Safety concerns: immune response to the vector, vector shedding, and insertion into host DNA.

Module 3 · Section 4

Identify the virus

Viruses are sorted by their nucleic acid, structure, and envelope. Seven medically important DNA families and fifteen RNA families sound like a lot, but a few questions narrow them quickly.

Key points

  • Viruses are categorized by nucleic acid, molecular weight, and structure: capsid, size, symmetry, and envelope.
  • +ssRNA genomes act as mRNA. −ssRNA and dsRNA viruses carry their own polymerase in the virion.
  • Influenza has a segmented genome and can reassort.
  • Hepadnavirus and poxvirus carry a polymerase in the virion. Poxvirus has complex symmetry; the other DNA virus families shown are icosahedral.

Follow It Through the Patient

A virus causes disease on two levels: it changes individual cells, and it changes the patient. The two do not always match. In this module
Module 4 · Section 1

What happens to the cell?

An infected cell has four possible fates. Each has a different mechanism, and each is tied to viruses you will meet again.

Key points

  • Death: host macromolecule synthesis is inhibited and the cell dies, while viral protein synthesis is unaffected. Example: poliovirus.
  • Fusion: membranes fuse into multinucleated giant cells, and cells may round up or darken (cytopathic effect, CPE). Examples: measles virus, RSV, HSV.
  • Transformation: tumor growth is promoted. Examples: HPV, EBV, HBV, HCV, HTLV-1.
  • No visible change: persistent or latent infection, where cell and virus coexist. Example: herpesvirus latency.

Module 4 · Section 2

Why does the patient feel sick?

The cell’s fate and the patient’s symptoms are different questions. Many symptoms come from dying cells, but others come from somewhere else.

Key points

  • Symptoms of many viral infections result from death of infected cells, which causes functional loss.
  • Rotavirus diarrhea: NSP4 (a viral enterotoxin) and serotonin from gut cells activate enteric nerves, increasing fluid secretion and gut motility.
  • Immunopathogenesis: the immune response itself causes symptoms.
  • Influenza: fever and muscle aches are driven by cytokines. Hepatitis B: liver injury is mainly caused by cytotoxic T cells.

Module 4 · Section 3

Local or systemic?

Where a virus goes decides where the disease appears, and how long the patient feels fine before it does.

Key points

  • Entry sites: respiratory tract, gut, skin or mucosa, bite, needle.
  • Local infection: the virus replicates and causes disease at or near the entry site. Examples: rhinovirus (nose and throat), rotavirus (gut lining), HPV (skin and mucosa).
  • Systemic infection: the virus spreads through lymph nodes into the blood (viremia) or along nerves to distant organs.
  • Examples: measles, poliovirus, and varicella-zoster virus have an incubation while the virus spreads. Rabies and herpesviruses travel along nerves.
  • Systemic spread usually means a longer incubation period and damage to distant organs. Antibodies, for example from vaccines, can stop a virus during viremia.

Module 5 · Section 1

The interferon alarm

An infected cell cannot always save itself, but it can warn its neighbors. Interferon is that warning, and the neighbors respond by making their own cells hostile to the virus.

Key points

  • Infected cells detect viral double-stranded RNA and release interferons.
  • Interferon-stimulated proteins then inhibit viral growth: RNase L degrades viral mRNA, and PKR phosphorylates eIF2-alpha and shuts down protein synthesis.
  • Interferons are classified by receptor: Type I (alpha, beta), Type II (gamma), Type III (lambda).
  • Nonspecific defenses include interferons and natural killer cells. Specific defenses are humoral and cell-mediated immunity.

Module 5 · Section 2

Nonspecific defenses

Before antibodies or T cells have been made, a set of fast, general defenses is already working. They do not need to recognize one particular virus.

Key points

  • Natural killer cells kill infected cells with perforin and granzymes (apoptosis).
  • Macrophages limit virus infection by phagocytosis.
  • Alpha-defensins are positively charged peptides that interfere with HIV binding its receptor.
  • APOBEC3G causes hypermutation of viral DNA.
  • Fever may destabilize enveloped viruses and may reduce replication. Mucociliary clearance moves pathogens out of the respiratory tract.
  • Host factors: neonates and older adults are most susceptible; higher-dose corticosteroids impair antiviral immunity; circumcision reduces the risk of HIV, HPV, and HSV-2 acquisition in men.

Module 5 · Section 3

Antibody defense

Antibodies are the body’s specific answer to a virion in the open. Their target is the same surface you built in Module 1.

Key points

  • Antibody production neutralizes virus infectivity by binding outer surface proteins.
  • Binding may prevent the virus from binding cell receptors.
  • Binding may cross-link the virus so the capsid cannot uncoat.
  • Binding may enhance phagocytosis.
  • Antibody can activate complement, which can lyse enveloped viruses.
  • IgA at mucosal surfaces (respiratory and gut) provides a first line of protection.

Module 5 · Section 4

Active, passive, or herd?

Protection can come from your own immune response, from someone else’s antibodies, or from the people around you.

Key points

  • Active immunity: protection by your own immune response, after infection or vaccination.
  • Passive immunity: protection by antibodies made by someone else, for example maternal IgG or immune globulin.
  • Herd immunity: when enough people are immune, a virus cannot spread easily, which also protects people who are not immune.

Module 5 · Section 5

CD8 or NK?

Antibodies cannot reach a virus hiding inside a cell. T cells and NK cells can, but they look for different signals on the infected cell’s surface.

Key points

  • An infected cell displays viral peptides on MHC class I.
  • A CD8 cytotoxic T cell recognizes the peptide–MHC I complex and releases perforin and granzymes, and the infected cell undergoes apoptosis.
  • CD4 helper T cells release cytokines that help B cells make antibody and help activate CD8 T cells.
  • Viruses that lower MHC class I escape CD8 T cells, but NK cells can detect the missing MHC I.

Module 5 · Section 6

Escape and persistence

Viruses evolve against the defenses you just met. Some block a single step. Some persist for years by combining several tricks.

Key points

  • Cytotoxic T cells: reduce MHC class I (HIV, HSV, CMV, adenovirus).
  • Helper (Th-1) T cells: block IL-12 (measles virus).
  • Interferon: block its synthesis (EBV), or block the kinase that phosphorylates initiation factor-2 (HIV, influenza, HSV).
  • Cytokines, chemokines, and complement: decoy receptors (vaccinia virus, CMV) and a C3b-binding protein (HSV).
  • In most cases the virus does not remain after recovery. Persistent infection can involve an integrated provirus, immune tolerance, infectious virus–antibody complexes, a sheltered site such as the brain, rapid antigenic variation, cell-to-cell spread, or immunosuppression.

Module 6 · Section 1

Choose the test

Five methods, five different questions. The skill is not memorizing the table; it is hearing the question and reaching for the right station.

Key points

  • Detecting viral nucleic acid: PCR or RT-PCR amplifies specific viral sequences. RNA levels for HIV and hepatitis C are used to monitor therapy.
  • Detecting viral antigens: ELISA is often used and is very sensitive. It may detect HIV antigen or hepatitis B surface antigen.
  • Serologic procedures: acute and convalescent samples are compared. A fourfold or greater rise in titer indicates recent infection.
  • Cell culture identification: viral growth in cells produces cytopathic effect (CPE).
  • Microscopic identification: light, fluorescence (immunofluorescence), or electron microscopy.

Module 6 · Section 2

Interpret the result

A result is only useful if you can read it. Each method has its own way of showing that a virus, or the response to it, is present.

Key points

  • CPE: change in size, shape, or fusion of cells indicates the type of virus.
  • Falling viral RNA levels show that therapy is lowering viral burden.
  • A titer is the highest serum dilution that is still positive. A fourfold or greater rise between acute and convalescent samples indicates recent infection.
  • Samples are collected about 2 to 4 weeks apart.
