Module 1

What makes a bacterium a bacterium?

Before a pharmacist can think about a drug, the first job is to place the microbe: what kind of agent is it, how does it differ from a human cell, and what does its outside look like?

In this module
Module 1 · Section 1

Microbes at a glance

Every infectious agent raises the same first questions: what is it made of, and how does it replicate? The answers decide which drugs can work at all, because a drug cannot hit a structure the microbe does not have.

Key points

  • Cells or not: prions and viruses are acellular, bacteria are prokaryotes, and fungi, protozoa, and helminths are eukaryotes.
  • Ribosomes give a quick divider: 70S in bacteria, 80S in eukaryotes, and none in prions or viruses.
  • Replication differs too: templated protein conversion, use of host machinery, binary fission, budding or mitosis, or complex life cycles.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Start with two questions: is it living, and what is unique about it? A prion offers no ribosome, wall, or nucleic acid for a drug to hit, which is why those two questions come before any thought about treatment.

Module 1 · Section 2

Bacterial vs. human cells

Bacterial and human cells share the basics, yet differ in the places drugs can exploit. Those differences aid in designing therapy and in anticipating side effects.

Key points

  • No membrane around the DNA, no mitosis, and no histone-packaged nucleosomes in bacteria.
  • No mitochondria or lysosomes in bacteria, and 70S rather than 80S cytosolic ribosomes.
  • Peptidoglycan is present in most bacteria and absent from human cells. Mycoplasma lack a wall, and some bacteria carry more than one chromosome.

The two cells share a plasma membrane, DNA, and ribosomes. The differences are where drugs can aim.

Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A shared structure predicts a shared risk. Both cell types have a plasma membrane, so a drug that attacks membranes has to be built to prefer the bacterial version, or it will injure the patient as well.

Module 1 · Section 3

Shape and arrangement

Shape comes from the cell wall. Arrangement comes from how cells stay together after they divide. Together they are the first identification clues on a Gram stain report.

Key points

  • Coccus is a sphere, bacillus is a rod, and spirochete is a spiral. The word “bacillus” can also be the genus Bacillus, so context tells you which is meant.
  • Prefixes describe arrangement: diplo- for pairs, strepto- for chains, and staphylo- for clusters.
  • Some bacteria are pleomorphic, meaning their shape varies.
Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Round cells stay attached after dividing and form chains. What are they called?

Which structure dictates a bacterium’s shape?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A Gram stain report is written in exactly this vocabulary, for example “Gram-positive cocci in clusters.” Reading that phrase fluently is the first step toward an empiric choice.

Module 1 · Section 4

Outside the cell wall

Everything outside the wall decides how a bacterium attaches, moves, and hides from the immune system. Open each part of the cell below to see what it does and why a pharmacist cares.

Key points

  • Glycocalyx is the broad term for the polymer layer outside the wall. A capsule is organized and tightly attached; a slime layer is diffuse and loosely attached.
  • The glycocalyx helps protect against desiccation, toxins, and phagocytosis.
  • Pili attach to host surfaces (adhesion), and flagella provide motility. Polysaccharides and proteins on the surface can serve as diagnostic antigens.

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Capsule polysaccharides do double duty. The structure that shields the bacterium from phagocytes also gives the immune system something to recognize, which is why capsular polysaccharides are used as antigens in bacterial vaccines.

Module 2

The envelope and selective toxicity

The bacterial envelope explains three things at once: how a Gram stain turns out, which drugs can get in, and why so many antibiotics spare the patient. This module builds that link.

Pharmacist’s questionHow do I treat the infection without harming the patient?
2
By the end you can
  • Explain how peptidoglycan is built, and why blocking its cross-links weakens the wall.
  • Compare Gram-positive and Gram-negative envelopes, and predict a Gram stain result from the structure.
  • Recognize organisms the Gram stain cannot classify, and name the alternative approach.
  • Match each antimicrobial class to the bacterial target it exploits, and explain why that target keeps the patient safe.
In this module
Module 2 · Section 1

Peptidoglycan: the wall

Peptidoglycan is the reason wall-targeting drugs are among the safest antibiotics: human cells do not make it. Its strength comes from cross-links, and cross-links are exactly what beta-lactams block.

Key points

  • Glycan chains alternate two sugars, NAG (N-acetylglucosamine) and NAM (N-acetylmuramic acid), joined by β-1,4 bonds.
  • Peptide bridges of about four to five amino acids cross-link the chains. Their sequence and length differ between bacteria.
  • Beta-lactam antibiotics block the transpeptidation (cross-linking) step.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Beta-lactams injure dividing bacteria yet spare human cells, because the step they block does not exist in the patient. A growing cell that is still building wall is the one most exposed when cross-linking stops.

Module 2 · Section 2

Gram-positive vs. Gram-negative

Two envelope designs explain Gram stain results, which drugs can enter, and where endotoxin is found. Compare them below, then sort features by design.

Key points

  • Gram-positive: a thick peptidoglycan wall that also contains teichoic acids. The exact thickness varies by species.
  • Gram-negative: a thin peptidoglycan layer plus an outer membrane that carries lipopolysaccharide (LPS).
  • Some bacteria fit neither class cleanly: Mycobacterium has an outer layer rich in mycolic acids.

Porins are channels in the outer membrane that admit small hydrophilic molecules, including some drugs. The thick wall of a Gram-positive cell carries no outer membrane.

Hands-on

Choose an answer for every feature, then check. Each row explains itself once checked.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

The outer membrane is a permeability barrier. Porins admit small hydrophilic molecules, but a large hydrophilic drug is largely kept out, which is a form of intrinsic resistance that no mutation created.

Module 2 · Section 3

The Gram stain

The Gram stain is a short chemical argument about wall structure. Watch what each reagent does to a thick-walled cell and a thin-walled cell with an outer membrane.

Key points

  • Gram-positive cells keep the crystal violet–iodine complex inside their thick peptidoglycan through the alcohol wash. Gram-negative cells lose it and take up the pink counterstain, safranin.
  • Gram-positive bacteria appear purple, and Gram-negative bacteria appear pink.
  • Interpretation combines the Gram reaction with morphology, arrangement, and specimen source.
Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A Gram stain shapes initial (empiric) therapy while cultures incubate, but it reports only shape, size, arrangement, and Gram reaction. It does not name the species, and it does not report susceptibility.

Module 2 · Section 4

When Gram staining falls short

Gram stains are among the most informative tests for identifying bacteria, but several important organisms do not stain reliably. Each one has a reason and a workaround.

Key points

  • Reasons include excess wall lipid, organisms too thin to see, no cell wall, poor counterstain uptake, and life inside host cells.
  • The workaround depends on the reason: a different stain, a different microscope, more counterstain time, or a search for inclusion bodies.
  • Modern practice increasingly relies on NAAT, antigen, and other molecular methods alongside or instead of these microscopic approaches.
Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

When a Gram stain is unhelpful, that is a clue in itself. A specimen suspected of Mycobacterium, Treponema, or Legionella calls for a different laboratory approach, so the request has to say so.

Module 2 · Section 5

Targeting the microbe, not the patient

Selective toxicity is the guiding idea of antimicrobial therapy: harm the microbe while sparing the patient. The safest target is one that exists in the microbe and has no counterpart in the patient.

Key points

  • Peptidoglycan (unique to bacteria): beta-lactams and glycopeptides.
  • 70S ribosomes (differ from human 80S): macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides.
  • Folate synthesis (bacteria make their own, humans take it in from the diet): sulfonamides and trimethoprim.
  • DNA gyrase and topoisomerase (bacterial enzymes): fluoroquinolones.
  • Bacterial membranes (structurally distinct from human membranes): daptomycin and polymyxins.
Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Ask the retrieval question the lecture ends on: why do beta-lactams generally harm bacteria more than human cells? The answer points to a target the patient lacks, or one that differs enough to be told apart.

Growth, survival, genetics, and resistance

Bacteria adapt quickly. This module follows an organism from the oxygen it needs, through the spores that let it wait out a disinfectant, to the DNA it swaps and the selection that turns one resistant cell into a resistant population.

In this module
Module 3 · Section 1

Oxygen and where bacteria grow

Oxygen requirement is a quick way to predict where an organism can live and which defenses it must carry. Make a prediction for each category before you see the tube.

Key points

  • Obligate aerobes require oxygen. Obligate anaerobes cannot grow with it. Facultative anaerobes can use oxygen but also grow without it, and most familiar clinical bacteria, such as E. coli, are in this group.
  • Microaerophiles need low oxygen. Aerotolerant anaerobes tolerate oxygen but do not use it for growth.
  • Aerobic growth gives a higher yield, but it requires enzymes that neutralize reactive oxygen species: typically superoxide dismutase and catalase.
  • Obligate intracellular pathogens (Chlamydia, Rickettsia, Ehrlichia, Anaplasma) need living host cells to grow.
Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Which enzyme pair neutralizes reactive oxygen species during aerobic growth?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Most familiar clinical bacteria are facultative anaerobes. The category matters when a specimen is collected and transported, because the organism you are trying to grow has to survive the trip.

Module 3 · Section 2

Endospores

Endospores are the reason C. difficile precautions differ from routine ones. They are dormant survival structures, not a way to multiply.

Key points

  • A single vegetative cell forms one spore, so sporulation does not increase cell numbers.
  • Spores are extremely resistant to heat, desiccation, radiation, and many disinfectants, and can persist in the environment for years.
  • Clostridioides (for example C. difficile) and Bacillus species are the key spore-formers for pharmacy practice. Not all bacteria form spores.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Alcohol-based hand rub does not kill spores. Soap-and-water handwashing, which removes them mechanically, is required, and sporicidal agents such as dilute bleach are needed for environmental disinfection.

Module 3 · Section 3

Moving DNA between bacteria

Bacteria change their DNA by mutation, by shuffling it within a genome, and by taking it from other cells. Three distinct mechanisms move DNA between cells. Step through each one.

Key points

  • Transposons move DNA from place to place within a genome, between the chromosome and plasmids.
  • Transformation is uptake of naked DNA. Transduction is transfer by a bacteriophage (a virus). Conjugation is direct cell-to-cell transfer by contact.
  • Conjugation uses a pilus as a bridge, and plasmids are the usual cargo. Plasmids can encode resistance genes, virulence genes such as exotoxins, metabolic traits, and transfer machinery.
Hands-on

Watch each mechanism, step by step

Choose a scene, then press Next step. Play all three scenes to the end to finish.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Which mechanism moves DNA directly between two cells that touch?

Which mechanism is uptake of naked DNA released from a dead cell?

Which element moves DNA between the chromosome and plasmids within one cell?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Resistant organisms can hand resistance genes to other bacteria, including bacteria that were never exposed to the drug. Conjugation on a plasmid is why resistance can spread so effectively.

Module 3 · Section 4

How resistance takes over

Antimicrobial exposure does not create resistance mutations. It selects for resistant cells that were already present, and removes the competition so they can multiply.

Key points

  • Intrinsic resistance is an inherent property, such as outer-membrane impermeability in Gram-negative bacteria.
  • Acquired resistance is gained through mutation or horizontal gene transfer.
  • Mutation and gene transfer generate or spread the resistance determinants; drug exposure sorts the population.
Hands-on

The selection lab

Make a prediction first. Then treat two populations with the same drug: one that contains a single resistant cell, and one that contains none.

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

Gram-negative bacilli resist a large hydrophilic drug because of outer-membrane impermeability. What type of resistance is this?

What does antibiotic exposure do to a mixed population?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

A resistant survivor can transfer its genes to other bacteria, for example by conjugation. Completing the course and choosing the drug thoughtfully both limit the number of rounds of selection a population goes through.

Module 4

From colonization to disease

Most bacteria a person meets never cause disease. This module explains the difference between living alongside a microbe and being harmed by one, and the tools bacteria use when they do cause harm.

  • Connect adherence, biofilm, spread, immune evasion, toxins, and immune-mediated damage to what happens in the patient.
In this module
Module 4 · Section 1

The human microbiome

The resident microbiota is the community of microbes that normally lives on and in the body. It is not just a passenger list: it shapes immunity, helps nutrition, and keeps pathogens out.

Key points

  • Members of the resident microbiota can act as mutualists, commensals, or potential pathogens depending on body site and host conditions.
  • The colon holds the majority of the microbiome, a dense and mostly anaerobic community. Healthy bladder urine and the lower airways are low-biomass, not sterile.
  • Colonization resistance is how the resident microbiota limits pathogens: competing for adhesion sites and nutrients, making antimicrobial metabolites and bacteriocins, and stimulating host defenses.

Module 4 · Section 2

Colonization, infection, and disease

These four words are easy to blur, and the difference decides whether a positive culture needs treatment. Sort the scenarios below.

Key points

  • Colonization: presence or growth of an organism without attributable tissue injury or disease.
  • Contamination: an organism from an external source, such as specimen handling, that was never growing in the patient.
  • Infection: the organism has invaded tissue and caused a host response, with or without symptoms.
  • Disease: symptomatic illness resulting from infection.
Hands-on

CAN YOU PREDICT IT?

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Ask the lecture’s question of every positive culture: does it justify an antibiotic on its own? Colonization and contamination usually need context before treatment, while infection with symptoms is where treatment decisions begin.

Module 4 · Section 3

How microbes travel

Transmission is the movement of a microbe from one place to another. The vocabulary is precise because the interventions differ: cleaning a fomite, controlling a vector, or finding the reservoir are different jobs.

Key points

  • Fomites are inanimate objects that transfer microbes, such as towels, bedsheets, and doorknobs. A vector is a living organism, usually an arthropod.
  • Vertical transmission is mother to child. Horizontal transmission is person to person, not via offspring.
  • The reservoir is the normal habitat that maintains and multiplies the agent. The source is the immediate origin of a specific exposure and may differ from the reservoir.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Module 4 · Section 4

Adherence, spread, and immune evasion

To cause harm, a bacterium has to stick, spread, and survive the immune response. Each step has a tool, and several of them are enzymes or surface structures you can name.

Key points

  • Adherence can lead to a biofilm: a protective matrix of proteins, polysaccharides, and extracellular DNA that may form on implanted devices and lowers antibiotic susceptibility.
  • Secreted enzymes promote spread: collagenase and hyaluronidase ease movement through tissue, coagulase promotes a fibrin clot that can shield bacteria, and IgA proteases cleave secretory IgA.
  • Evasion tools: a capsule blocks phagocyte attachment, M protein and protein A interfere with complement and antibody, and leukocidins destroy phagocytes. Opsonization marks a bacterium for phagocytosis.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

Module 4 · Section 5

Toxins and immune-mediated damage

Not all damage comes from the organism itself. Some comes from a secreted protein, some from a component of the envelope, and some from the host’s own response.

Key points

  • Exotoxins are secreted polypeptides made by Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Some diseases, such as tetanus and botulism, are toxin-mediated without substantial tissue invasion.
  • Endotoxin is lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. It is built from lipid A, a core oligosaccharide, and the O antigen, in that order outward. Lipid A is the major inflammatory component.
  • In immunopathogenesis the immune response, not the organism, causes symptoms. Rheumatic fever is an example.
Hands-on

PHARMACIST’S LENS

The category tells you what prevention looks like. A toxin-mediated disease can be prevented with a toxoid vaccine, while an endotoxin is weakly antigenic and is not used to make toxoids.

Module 5 · Section 1

The diagnostic sequence

Each step in the sequence answers a different question, and each depends on the one before it. A poor specimen damages everything that follows.

Key points

  • Specimen quality is the foundation for every downstream result.
  • Gram stain gives shape, size, arrangement, and Gram reaction, and guides initial therapy while cultures incubate.
  • Culture, or NAAT and antigen testing, comes next, followed by identification, susceptibility testing, and clinical interpretation.

Module 5 · Section 2

Reading culture results

Organism detection alone does not automatically establish infection. The site, the number of positive samples, and the patient all change what the same result means.

Key points

  • A normally sterile site, such as cerebrospinal fluid, makes any growth significant.
  • Growth from a non-sterile site, or in only one of several samples, needs the clinical picture.
  • Toxin-based results are read together with symptoms and recent antibiotic exposure.

Module 5 · Section 3

MIC and susceptibility

The minimum inhibitory concentration is a laboratory measurement, and the S, I, and R categories translate it into a likely response. Move the slider to see how one number becomes a report.

Key points

  • MIC is the lowest antimicrobial concentration that visibly inhibits growth in the laboratory.
  • Susceptible (S): likely to respond to standard dosing. Intermediate or increased exposure (I): may respond at higher dosing or where the drug concentrates. Resistant (R): unlikely to respond even at maximal safe dosing.
  • MIC and susceptibility describe the organism in a test tube. They do not account for biofilms, source control, or drug penetration at the infection site.

PHARMACIST’S LENS
